# Part One: Debate Outline

---

## Domain & Evidence

This debate is exegetical before it is theological. Both Judaism and Christianity treat the Hebrew Bible as authoritative divine Scripture. The dispute is not about whether these texts carry weight but about what they say: what the authors of Isaiah 53, Daniel 9, and Psalm 22 meant, what their original audiences could have understood, and whether those texts point toward Jesus of Nazareth.

The primary evidence is the Hebrew text of the Tanakh, read alongside Second Temple Jewish interpretive tradition, early rabbinic commentary, the New Testament's use of these passages, and the Dead Sea Scrolls, which in some cases give access to pre-Christian Jewish interpretive patterns. Secondary evidence includes comparative exegesis across the targums, the Septuagint, the Peshitta, and later medieval Jewish exegetes such as Rashi and Maimonides. The historical question (whether Jesus fulfilled specific textual expectations) is inseparable from the exegetical question of what those expectations were.

The central disputed questions are: whether the Servant of Isaiah 52-53 is the nation of Israel or an individual, whether the Danielic "seventy weeks" chronology maps onto Jesus, whether Psalm 22 is a general lament repurposed as prophecy or a specific prediction, and whether the New Testament's christological reading of these texts represents fulfillment, creative reinterpretation, or misappropriation of meaning. Each passage carries its own exegetical disputes, and those disputes are not independent of each other. The answers tend to cluster around prior hermeneutical commitments about the nature of prophecy and fulfillment.

---

## Key Definitions

### God

**Judaism** holds that God is absolutely one, without partners, without division, and without inner personal distinctions that would compromise his unity. The Shema (Deut. 6:4) is the formal center of this commitment, and nothing in Judaism's interpretive tradition qualifies it.

**Christianity** holds that God is one in being and three in person (Father, Son, and Holy Spirit), and that this tri-personal unity is disclosed in the history of Jesus and the coming of the Spirit, not invented by later theology.

### Messiah

**Judaism** holds that the Messiah (mashiach, "anointed one") is a future human king from the line of David who will fulfill specific, publicly verifiable criteria: rebuilding the Temple, gathering all Jews to Israel, ushering in universal peace, and bringing all nations to knowledge of the one God. These are historical, geopolitical events. Since Jesus did not accomplish them, his messianic claim fails on Judaism's own criteria. The Messiah does not die before completing his mission; the concept of a "dying Messiah" requires textual warrant Judaism denies.

**Christianity** holds that Jesus is the Messiah: he fulfilled the Davidic covenant through incarnation, inaugurated the kingdom of God through death and resurrection, and will complete remaining messianic expectations at his return. Christianity reframes fulfillment as two-stage (first and second coming) and interprets many prophetic texts as fulfilled spiritually or inaugurated rather than geopolitically completed.

### Prophecy and Fulfillment

**Judaism** generally treats prophecy as describing actual future historical events. A prophecy is fulfilled when its stated conditions are met in the observable world. Predictive prophecy about the Messiah sets criteria the Messiah must visibly satisfy. If the criteria are not met, the prophecy remains unfulfilled and the candidate fails. Spiritualized or typological fulfillment is considered a later and foreign hermeneutic imposed on texts that meant something concrete.

**Christianity** holds that prophecy operates typologically and intertextually. Earlier patterns are recapitulated and intensified in later events, which can fulfill literal sense, typological sense, or both. This does not make fulfillment arbitrary; the relationship between promise and fulfillment is complex and must be traced through the whole canonical narrative. Jesus' use of the Psalms and Isaiah, and Paul's exegetical practice, are treated as authorized models for this kind of reading.

### The Suffering Servant (Isaiah 52-53)

**Judaism** historically has understood the Servant as the nation of Israel collectively: Israel suffering unjustly at the hands of the nations, with the nations eventually acknowledging that Israel's suffering was undeserved. The medieval shift to this reading was partly in response to Christian use of the passage, but collective and national readings have pre-Christian antecedents in the targums and some Second Temple literature.

**Christianity** holds that the Servant is an individual whose death is explicitly said to bear the sins of others, who is led like a lamb to slaughter, who makes his grave with the wicked and the rich, and who sees the result of his suffering and is exalted. This individual reading was standard in early Christianity and, with some variation, in certain pre-Christian Jewish interpretations.

### Daniel's Seventy Weeks (Daniel 9:24-27)

**Judaism** interprets the seventy-weeks prophecy as referring to events surrounding the Maccabean crisis and Antiochus IV Epiphanes, or as a schematic account of the period from Babylonian exile to the destruction of the Second Temple. The "anointed one who is cut off" (v. 26) is commonly identified with the high priest Onias III, assassinated in 171 BCE.

**Christianity** calculates the seventy weeks as pointing to the first-century ministry and death of Jesus, using either the decree of Artaxerxes in Nehemiah 2 (445/444 BCE) or Ezra 7 (458/457 BCE) as the starting point, arriving through various calculations at the time of Jesus' public ministry and crucifixion.

### Psalm 22

**Judaism** reads Psalm 22 as a personal lament of David, describing his own distress and his trust in God despite apparent abandonment. The language is intense and poetic. The verse "they have pierced my hands and my feet" (v. 16) is contested at the textual level: the Hebrew word is disputed, with some manuscripts reading *ka'ari* ("like a lion") rather than *karu* ("they have pierced"). The psalm ends in vindication and praise, with David's God praised among the nations.

**Christianity** reads the psalm as a Davidic lament that typologically anticipates and is applied to Jesus at the crucifixion. The opening cry of dereliction ("My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?") is quoted by Jesus from the cross, the mocking language matches the Gospel accounts, and the lot-casting over clothing corresponds to the soldiers at Golgotha. The New Testament treats this as deliberate typological fulfillment, not coincidence.

## Foundational Assumptions

### Position A assumes

Judaism's interpretive tradition operates on the premise that messianic prophecy describes concrete historical deliverances, and that reading those prophecies spiritually or typologically is an accommodation invented after the fact to rescue a candidate who did not satisfy the plain sense. A second assumption is that the interpretive tradition preserved in Oral Torah, Mishnah, Talmud, and rabbinic exegesis is a reliable guide to what these texts were understood to mean within their own community. A third assumption is that "fulfilled prophecy" arguments require a degree of specificity and verifiability that allows independent confirmation, meaning a genuine prophecy must be distinguishable from a text being applied to events after the fact.

### Position B assumes

Christianity's reading assumes that the New Testament's use of the Hebrew Bible is not arbitrary but reflects an interpretive logic present in Second Temple exegesis: reading earlier texts in light of later events, treating patterns as typologically significant, and finding in Jesus the recapitulation and intensification of Israel's story. A second assumption is that Jesus and the NT authors had sufficient access to and standing within the Jewish interpretive tradition to read these texts the way they did, and that their readings are not foreign impositions but extensions of existing interpretive methods. A third assumption is that the cumulative weight of multiple passages, rather than any single proof text, constitutes the evidential case, and that the convergence of the Servant, the Danielic anointed one, and the Psalm 22 pattern on a single figure strengthens the claim even when any individual text admits of alternative readings.

# Position A: Judaism: Strongest Case

---

## Argument 1: The Suffering Servant Is Israel, Not an Individual Messiah

The Servant Songs in Isaiah (42, 49, 50, 52-53) appear in a book where Israel is already explicitly called God's servant multiple times (Isa. 41:8-9; 44:1-2; 45:4; 49:3). The most natural reading of the Servant in 52-53 is continuity with those earlier identifications. The nations who speak the "we" of Isaiah 53:1-6 are the Gentile nations who acknowledge that they persecuted Israel unjustly and that Israel's suffering was innocent, not deserved punishment for their own sins. The corporate reading does not require a strained interpretation; it requires reading the passage within its own immediate literary context.

> **P1:** Isaiah identifies Israel explicitly as God's servant at multiple points in chapters 40-49.
>
> **P2:** The Servant Songs are embedded in that same section, with 49:3 explicitly equating the Servant with Israel.
>
> **P3:** Reading the Servant of 52-53 as Israel is consistent with the dominant pattern established by the surrounding text.
>
> **C:** The corporate, national reading of the Suffering Servant is the contextually grounded default.

**Critique 1:** Isaiah 49:3-6 creates a tension within the corporate reading: the Servant is called Israel in v. 3 but is then given a mission *to* Israel in vv. 5-6, suggesting a distinction between the Servant and the nation as a whole. An individual within or on behalf of Israel fits this structure better than a pure corporate identification.

**Critique 2:** Isaiah 53 attributes to the Servant an entirely innocent death that is effective for others ("he bore the sin of many," 53:12). If the Servant is Israel, Israel must be entirely innocent and must have died vicariously for the Gentile nations, neither of which is consistent with how the prophets generally describe Israel's situation.

**Rebuttal 1:** The distinction in 49:5-6 can be resolved by reading the Servant as the faithful remnant within Israel, a subset of the nation carrying out Israel's original vocation, not a single individual. This explains the internal differentiation without requiring the text to jump to an individual messiah nowhere else identified in Isaiah.

**Rebuttal 2:** "Innocence" in the context of Isaiah 40-55 refers to Israel's suffering in exile being disproportionate to their sin. God has already "double-punished" them (Isa. 40:2). The Servant's undeserved suffering is Israel's exile read through the lens of radical injustice acknowledged by the nations.

---

## Argument 2: Daniel 9's Chronology Fits the Maccabean Crisis Better Than the First Century

The most natural terminus a quo for the seventy weeks in Daniel 9:25 is the decree to Jeremiah mentioned in the surrounding context (v. 2), i.e., Jeremiah's seventy-year oracle. The "anointed leader" of v. 25 is then Zerubbabel or Cyrus, the "anointed one cut off" in v. 26 is Onias III (murdered 171 BCE), and the desolating abomination is Antiochus IV's desecration of the Temple (167 BCE). These events are described elsewhere in Daniel (ch. 8, 11) and fit the Maccabean context with precision that does not require calendrical adjustments.

> **P1:** Daniel 9's immediate literary context invokes Jeremiah's seventy-year oracle, providing an internal starting point for the calculation.
>
> **P2:** The events of the Maccabean crisis (the cutting off of Onias III and the Antiochene desolation) correspond to the specific events described in vv. 26-27 with historical precision.
>
> **P3:** Christian calculations require selecting among multiple possible decrees, adjusting calendar systems, and using varying year-lengths to arrive at a first-century date.
>
> **C:** The Maccabean reading fits Daniel 9 without requiring the external adjustments the Christian reading depends on.

**Critique 1:** The problem with the Maccabean reading is that Onias III is an obscure figure to whom the text gives enormous weight. If he is the anointed one cut off, the passage's climax concerns a historical assassination that had no lasting theological significance and that is otherwise treated as background noise in the broader Maccabean literature.

**Critique 2:** Daniel 9:24 lists six goals to be accomplished within the seventy weeks: finishing transgression, ending sin, atoning for iniquity, bringing in everlasting righteousness, sealing vision and prophecy, and anointing the most holy. None of these were accomplished by the Maccabean period. Sin continued, the Temple was rededicated but not eschatologically restored, and the prophetic era was widely thought to have continued.

**Rebuttal 1:** Daniel is apocalyptic literature. Its purposes are encouragement and theodicy for communities under persecution, not calendrical precision about remote future events. The six goals of v. 24 describe an eschatological horizon that the passage brackets, not a checklist completed in the first century either.

**Rebuttal 2:** Christian fulfilled-prophecy readings of Daniel face the same objection: Jesus' first coming did not bring in everlasting righteousness, seal prophecy, or accomplish a restoration of the Temple. Christians must also defer some items to a second coming, which means both readings handle the incompleteness similarly.

---

## Argument 3: Psalm 22 Is a Davidic Lament, Not a Messianic Prediction

Psalm 22 opens in the first-person voice of David expressing acute personal suffering: abandonment, mockery, physical distress, enemies closing in. The vocabulary is consistent with lament psalm conventions throughout the Psalter. There is no announcement formula ("thus says the Lord concerning the Messiah"), no future-tense prediction, and no explicit messianic marker that distinguishes this psalm from the many other lament psalms. The NT's use of it is typological retrofitting: once the crucifixion had occurred, early Christians recognized correspondences between the psalm and the passion narrative and treated those correspondences as fulfillment.

> **P1:** Psalm 22 has no internal predictive markers (no prophetic announcement, no future-tense framing, no statement that it concerns a future figure).
>
> **P2:** Its conventions (first-person lament, description of distress, divine vindication) are standard across the Psalter and do not distinguish it as uniquely messianic.
>
> **P3:** The text-critical dispute over v. 16 ("pierced" vs. "like a lion") means the most cited "fulfilled" detail rests on a contested reading.
>
> **C:** Reading Psalm 22 as predictive messianic prophecy requires importing interpretive assumptions that the text itself does not supply.

**Critique 1:** The "no prediction markers" argument proves too much. If the absence of explicit prophetic framing disqualifies a text from typological or messianic reading, it also disqualifies much of what the prophets themselves treated as pointing forward. Second Temple interpreters regularly found messianic significance in psalms without internal predictive markers.

**Critique 2:** Jesus' quotation of Psalm 22:1 from the cross is one of the most securely attested sayings in the passion tradition. His deliberate invocation of the psalm (within a Jewish context where citing an opening verse evokes the whole psalm) is evidence for how he understood his own situation, regardless of what the original Davidic context was.

**Rebuttal 1:** Second Temple messianic exegesis is diverse and often sectarian. Finding messianic significance in a psalm does not establish that the psalm *predicted* a specific historical individual; it establishes that certain communities applied it that way, which is a different claim.

**Rebuttal 2:** The text-critical dispute over v. 16 is not a minor quibble. The word in question is the verse most cited as a specific physical prediction of crucifixion. If the better-attested Hebrew reading is *ka'ari* ("like a lion"), the passage describes ferocious enemies, not piercing, and the case for specific prediction weakens significantly.

## Pressure Points for Judaism

### Argument 1: Pre-Christian Jewish Sources Read Isaiah 53 Individually

The claim that the corporate reading of Isaiah 53 is the natural Jewish reading faces a complication: pre-Christian and early post-Christian Jewish sources contain individual readings of the passage. The Targum of Jonathan, while ultimately translating the Servant as Israel, shows signs of earlier individual readings layered beneath its corporate interpretation. The Dead Sea Scrolls community at Qumran appears to have applied Servant language to their Teacher of Righteousness. Several Talmudic passages refer the passage to individual messianic figures, including the Messiah ben Joseph and the Messiah ben David. The rabbinic consensus on the corporate reading solidifies primarily in the medieval period, partly in response to Christian polemical use.

> **P1:** Individual readings of Isaiah 53 appear in Jewish sources that predate or are contemporaneous with early Christianity.
>
> **P2:** The Talmud contains references that apply the Servant's suffering to messianic figures.
>
> **P3:** The dominant corporate reading does not appear as a uniform consensus until the medieval period.
>
> **C:** Judaism's current interpretive consensus on Isaiah 53 is the endpoint of a development, not the original and obvious reading that Christianity displaced.

**Critique 1:** Diversity of interpretation within Jewish sources does not validate any particular reading. The existence of individual readings in some sources shows that the text was contested, not that the Christian reading is correct.

**Critique 2:** Talmudic references that apply the passage to messianic suffering do not specify Jesus, and the figures named (Messiah ben Joseph, etc.) fit a very different narrative than the one Christianity describes.

**Rebuttal 1:** Those points reduce the strength of the argument but do not remove it. The relevant claim is the narrower one: that the corporate reading was not so obvious within Judaism's own tradition that the Christian individual reading requires external imposition to explain it.

**Rebuttal 2:** If even Jewish interpreters working within Second Temple and rabbinic tradition found individual readings plausible, Christianity's individual reading cannot be dismissed as a Christian invention foreign to the text's Jewish context.

### Argument 2: The Absence of Fulfilled Geopolitical Criteria Is Theologically Significant

Judaism's messianic criteria are not arbitrary checklists; they are the substance of the prophetic hope: the restoration of Israel, universal peace, the nations acknowledging the God of Israel, the end of idolatry, the rebuilding of the Temple. These are conditions that, once met, would be publicly visible to everyone. They are not the kind of events that could be "spiritually fulfilled" or deferred to a second coming without changing what the prophets actually said. If a candidate fails the criteria, Judaism's tradition holds that the honest response is to look elsewhere.

> **P1:** The prophetic texts that describe the Messiah's work specify outcomes that are publicly observable: political restoration, universal peace, Temple rebuilding, recognition of Israel's God by all nations.
>
> **P2:** These conditions were not met in the first century by any measurable standard.
>
> **P3:** Deferring unmet criteria to a second coming requires the prophecies to mean something other than what they plainly say.
>
> **C:** On Judaism's interpretive standard for prophecy (concrete, public, verifiable fulfillment) Jesus does not qualify.

**Critique 1:** Christianity does not claim that all prophetic expectations were exhausted in the first century. The two-advent structure is exegetically argued, not simply asserted. Isaiah itself describes both a Servant who suffers and an eschatological restoration that postdates the Servant's work.

**Critique 2:** Judaism's own messianic tradition also defers and restages expectations. The concept of Messiah ben Joseph (who suffers and dies) followed by Messiah ben David (who conquers) is itself a two-stage structure developed to account for prophetic texts that seemed to require both a suffering and a triumphant Messiah.

**Rebuttal 1:** The two-advent structure is a Christian reading of texts that, in their original sense, describe a single figure accomplishing a coherent sequence of events. Splitting those events across two advents separated by two millennia (and counting) requires significant interpretive warrant.

**Rebuttal 2:** The Messiah ben Joseph / ben David distinction shows Judaism itself faced the same tension, but it resolves that tension through two distinct figures, not two appearances of the same figure after a long gap.

# Position B: Christianity: Strongest Case

---

## Argument 1: Isaiah 53 Describes an Individual Whose Death Is Vicarious and Effective

The text of Isaiah 52:13-53:12 describes a figure whose suffering is explicitly said to be on behalf of others. The speakers use first-person plural throughout: "he bore *our* sicknesses," "he was wounded for *our* transgressions," "the punishment that brought *us* peace was upon him," "the LORD has laid on him the iniquity of *us all*." If the Servant is Israel, then Israel is suffering on behalf of Israel: the collective substituting for itself. This creates an incoherent substitution structure. An individual bearing the sins of others produces a coherent substitution; a collective bearing its own sins does not.

> **P1:** Isaiah 53 uses consistent first-person plural language to describe the beneficiaries of the Servant's suffering as a group distinct from the Servant.
>
> **P2:** If the Servant is Israel, the speakers who say "we esteemed him not" and "he bore our sins" are also Israel, which means Israel is substituting for itself.
>
> **P3:** Coherent substitutionary logic requires a distinction between the one who suffers and the ones whose suffering is thereby borne.
>
> **C:** The substitution language in Isaiah 53 points to an individual Servant acting on behalf of others, not a collective acting on behalf of itself.

**Critique 1:** The corporate reading handles this by distinguishing between different parts of Israel: the faithful remnant suffering on behalf of the whole nation, or Israel suffering on behalf of the Gentile nations who then acknowledge the injustice. The "we" who speak can be Gentile nations, not other Israelites.

**Critique 2:** Isaiah uses corporate-singular language throughout chapters 40-55 for Israel, which grammatically allows the Servant to function as a collective entity described with singular pronouns and individual imagery.

**Rebuttal 1:** If the speakers are Gentile nations, the nations are confessing that Israel suffered for the nations' transgressions, a claim with no parallel elsewhere in the prophets and no development in Second Temple literature as a doctrine of Gentile atonement through Israel's suffering.

**Rebuttal 2:** Corporate-singular usage is real in biblical Hebrew, but it does not dissolve the problem. The passage attributes to the Servant specific individual features (being brought as a lamb to slaughter, making his grave with the wicked and the rich, seeing his offspring after death) that function poorly as descriptions of a corporate entity.

## Argument 2: The Danielic Chronology Converges on the First Century

Daniel 9:25 specifies that from the issuing of a decree to restore and rebuild Jerusalem to the coming of an anointed one will be seven weeks plus sixty-two weeks (sixty-nine weeks of years, or 483 years). Using the decree of Artaxerxes to Nehemiah in 445/444 BCE as the terminus a quo and a 360-day prophetic year (attested in apocalyptic usage), the calculation arrives at the approximate period of Jesus' public ministry and entry into Jerusalem. The coincidence of a specific chronological calculation in a major prophetic text with the life of the figure Christianity identifies as Messiah is not trivially explained.

> **P1:** Daniel 9:25 provides a specific chronological marker: from a decree concerning Jerusalem's restoration to the coming of an anointed one.
>
> **P2:** Using the Artaxerxes-to-Nehemiah decree as the starting point and prophetic-year reckoning, the calculation arrives at the late Second Temple period.
>
> **P3:** No other historical figure has been proposed who fits both the chronological and the anointed-one criteria in this window.
>
> **C:** Daniel 9 points to the first century as the expected time of the Messiah's arrival, which is where Jesus appears.

**Critique 1:** The calculation requires choosing among multiple possible decrees, selecting a particular calendar system, and allowing adjustments of years to achieve the result. These choices are not internally mandated by the text.

**Critique 2:** Even Talmudic sources express awareness that the messianic timetable pointed to the first century. Rabbi Akiva's support for Bar Kokhba notwithstanding, there is a famous Talmudic statement tracking with first-century messianic expectation. The chronological pressure in the first century was real, but it produced multiple messianic claimants, not just Jesus.

**Rebuttal 1:** The selection of the Artaxerxes-Nehemiah decree is not arbitrary. It is the only decree in the Nehemiah-Ezra corpus that specifically concerns the restoration of the city, which is what Daniel 9:25 specifies. The calendar choice is debatable but not invented.

**Rebuttal 2:** Multiple messianic claimants in the first century confirm the chronological pressure but do not settle which claimant was correct. Christianity argues Jesus is distinguished by resurrection, not merely by timing.

---

## Argument 3: Psalm 22 Was Deliberately Invoked by Jesus and Fits the Passion with Unusual Specificity

Jesus' citation of Psalm 22:1 from the cross is among the most difficult sayings in the passion tradition to explain as an invention. It is not the kind of triumphalist last word a community fabricating a narrative would choose. The saying expresses real abandonment and distress, which creates a theological problem the early church then had to handle. This suggests the tradition is early and not shaped for apologetic convenience. Beyond v. 1, the convergence of the psalm's details (the mocking of a righteous sufferer, the encirclement by enemies described as a pack, the lot-casting over clothing) with the Gospel passion accounts is too specific to be dismissed as generic lament pattern.

> **P1:** Jesus' citation of Psalm 22:1 from the cross is historically early and creates a theological difficulty for early Christianity rather than resolving one, which argues against invention.
>
> **P2:** The psalm's specific imagery (mockery of a righteous sufferer, encirclement by enemies, lot-casting over clothing) corresponds in detail to the Gospel passion accounts.
>
> **P3:** Jesus' deliberate invocation of the psalm within a Jewish context that treated opening-verse citation as invoking the whole psalm signals that he understood his death through the lens of this text.
>
> **C:** Psalm 22 was actively interpreted by Jesus himself as structuring his passion, and the correspondences are too specific to reduce to generic lament convention.

**Critique 1:** The passion narrative was written after the crucifixion. Gospel authors familiar with Psalm 22 could have shaped the narrative details (the mocking language, the lot-casting) to match the psalm, rather than the psalm predicting those details.

**Critique 2:** Jesus' citation of v. 1 could be a genuine cry of dereliction drawing on the language of Jewish lament without implying that the whole psalm is being consciously invoked as predictive prophecy about himself.

**Rebuttal 1:** The lot-casting detail is in John 19:24, where the evangelist explicitly notes the fulfillment. The crucifixion itself is multiply attested and the lot-casting tradition is not obviously constructed for apologetic purposes.

**Rebuttal 2:** In Jewish interpretive practice of the period, citing an incipit was understood to invoke the whole text. If Jesus said "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me," he was invoking Psalm 22 (not just its opening verse), and the psalm as a whole ends in vindication and public proclamation among the nations, which is precisely the structure of the Christian resurrection proclamation.

## Pressure Points for Christianity

### Argument 1: Typological Fulfillment Is Difficult to Distinguish from Retrofitting

Christianity's reading of these passages relies heavily on typological interpretation: the claim that earlier texts foreshadow later events in ways that constitute genuine fulfillment even when the original text did not explicitly predict those events. This is a real and ancient interpretive method, but it creates an epistemological problem. Once typological correspondence is accepted as sufficient for "fulfillment," it is not clear what would count as non-fulfillment. Any narrative of suffering, divine vindication, and universal impact could be claimed to fulfill Isaiah 53. The method needs principled constraints to be evidentially useful.

> **P1:** Typological fulfillment depends on recognizing correspondences between earlier texts and later events.
>
> **P2:** Without principled constraints on which correspondences count and why, the method can be applied selectively to support any predetermined candidate.
>
> **P3:** Christianity identifies Jesus as the Servant and then reads the passion narrative in light of Isaiah 53, which makes it difficult to evaluate the fit independently.
>
> **C:** Christianity needs to articulate what would count as a non-fulfillment of these passages, or the fulfillment claim is not falsifiable.

**Critique 1:** The constraint Christianity appeals to is not arbitrary correspondence but the canonical narrative taken as a whole: the pattern of Israel's story, the Davidic covenant, the Servant Songs, and the resurrection of Jesus must cohere. This is not the same as matching any single detail.

**Critique 2:** The Dead Sea Scrolls show that pre-Christian Jewish communities also engaged in typological and pesher interpretation, applying prophetic texts to their own community's present experience. Christianity's interpretive method is not foreign to Second Temple Judaism.

**Rebuttal 1:** Coherence with a canonical narrative is a real constraint, but it is an internal constraint. It shows the Christian reading is internally consistent, not that it is externally correct.

**Rebuttal 2:** Qumran's pesher method was also criticized for selectivity by outside observers. The existence of the method in Second Temple Judaism normalizes it but does not validate any particular application of it.

### Argument 2: The Two-Advent Structure Has No Explicit Basis in the Texts Cited

Christianity's response to unfulfilled messianic criteria is to distinguish between first-advent fulfillment (atonement, resurrection, inauguration of the kingdom) and second-advent fulfillment (geopolitical restoration, universal peace, Temple, universal acknowledgment of God). This distinction allows Christianity to claim Isaiah 53, Daniel 9, and Psalm 22 while deferring the public, visible criteria to a future return. But the texts themselves do not describe a gap between a suffering phase and a restoring phase separated by millennia. The two-advent structure is a hermeneutical framework brought to the texts, not derived from them.

> **P1:** The prophetic texts cited in messianic arguments do not themselves describe a two-phase fulfillment separated by a long interval.
>
> **P2:** The second-advent deferral allows Christianity to claim fulfillment for whatever Jesus accomplished while deferring to the future whatever he did not.
>
> **P3:** A framework that can defer unfulfilled predictions indefinitely does not give prophecy the predictive force that fulfilled-prophecy arguments require.
>
> **C:** Christianity's two-advent framework protects the messianic claim from falsification at the cost of reducing the prophetic evidence to something less than prediction.

**Critique 1:** Isaiah itself contains both suffering-Servant and triumphant-restoration material in sequences that are not described as simultaneous. The idea that fulfillment comes in stages is not foreign to the prophets; it is present in the texts.

**Critique 2:** The resurrection of Jesus is a public historical claim that Christianity treats as the pivot of the entire framework. If the resurrection stands, the two-advent structure has a historically grounded hinge rather than being an ad hoc adjustment.

**Rebuttal 1:** Staged fulfillment within Isaiah addresses the internal ordering of prophetic material, but it does not supply a two-millennia gap as a feature of that staging. The gap is assumed from the Christian historical situation, not read off the text.

**Rebuttal 2:** The resurrection is the hinge Christianity needs, but it is also the point Judaism contests. The fulfilled-prophecy arguments and the resurrection argument need to be run separately. Using the resurrection to anchor the two-advent framework, while also using the two-advent framework to protect the fulfilled-prophecy claims, creates circularity if both are used to establish the messianic case simultaneously.

# The Three Actual Hinges

The real issue is not whether Isaiah 53, Daniel 9, and Psalm 22 are important texts; both traditions treat them as significant. The real issue is **what the Servant's identity requires**. If the substitution language in Isaiah 53 requires an individual, and if that individual's suffering is vicarious and effective in a way that applies to the sins of others, then the text demands a figure whose suffering accomplishes something that Israel's collective exile did not. Judaism needs to show how a collective can function as a coherent subject of substitutionary language. Christianity needs to show that Jesus is the individual the passage describes and not simply a candidate selected to fit a text.

A second hinge is **whether the messianic criteria admit of a two-stage structure**. This is not merely an abstract interpretive question; it determines whether the criteria in the prophets can be used as a test at all. If criteria can be deferred indefinitely to a second coming, they lose evidential weight. Judaism's insistence on publicly verifiable fulfillment sets a higher evidentiary standard. Christianity's response is that the resurrection creates a different evidentiary base, one that the prophets did not explicitly describe but that the texts, read canonically, are compatible with. Whether that compatibility rises to the level of prediction is what separates the two positions.

A third hinge is **the text-critical status of Psalm 22:16**. The Hebrew word *ka'ari* / *karu* determines whether the most cited physical detail of crucifixion prediction is actually present in the text or is an artifact of the Septuagint translation and later tradition. The Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran supports a reading closer to "they have dug" or "pierced," which aligns with the Christian reading. The Masoretic tradition in most manuscripts reads *ka'ari*, "like a lion." This is a textual dispute, not merely a theological one, and its resolution affects how much weight the "specific physical prediction" argument carries.

## Jewish sources and scholarship

The Hebrew Bible (Tanakh). *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia*. Stuttgart: Deutsche Bibelgesellschaft, 1977/1997. Standard critical edition of the Masoretic Text.

*The Dead Sea Scrolls Bible*. Translated and edited by Martin Abegg Jr., Peter Flint, and Eugene Ulrich. New York: HarperOne, 1999. Includes the Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa^a), relevant to the text-critical question at Psalm 22:16 and the Isaiah 53 tradition.

Rashi (Solomon ben Isaac). Commentary on Isaiah. In *Mikraot Gedolot*. Standard rabbinic Bible with medieval commentaries. Rashi's reading of Isaiah 53 as referring to Israel remains the dominant traditional Jewish interpretation.

Maimonides (Moses ben Maimon). *Mishneh Torah*, Laws of Kings 11-12. On messianic criteria: what the Messiah must accomplish and the conditions for declaring a candidate's failure.

Ibn Ezra, Abraham. Commentary on Isaiah. Ibn Ezra shows awareness of the individual reading of Isaiah 53 while ultimately rejecting it, giving access to the exegetical issues as seen from within the medieval Jewish tradition.

*The Targum of Isaiah*. Translated by Bruce D. Chilton. Edinburgh: T&T Clark, 1987. The Aramaic targum of Isaiah 52-53 applies Servant language to a messianic figure in ways that complicate a purely corporate reading.

Levenson, Jon D. *The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son: The Transformation of Child Sacrifice in Judaism and Christianity*. New Haven: Yale University Press, 1993. On the broader pattern of vicarious suffering and death in Hebrew Bible and Jewish tradition.

Berger, David. *The Rebbe, the Messiah, and the Scandal of Orthodox Indifference*. Oxford: Littman Library of Jewish Civilization, 2001. Jewish messianic criteria and their application in recent history; provides a clear account of what Judaism requires of a messianic candidate.

Novak, David. *Jewish-Christian Dialogue: A Jewish Justification*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1989. Theological framework for the dialogue from a traditional Jewish perspective.

Katz, Steven T. *The Cambridge History of Judaism*. Vol. 4: *The Late Roman-Rabbinic Period*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2006. Historical context for the development of rabbinic messianism.

## Christian sources and scholarship

The Bible. *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia* (Hebrew) and *Novum Testamentum Graece*, 28th ed. (Nestle-Aland). Stuttgart: Deutsche Bibelgesellschaft, 2012.

Justin Martyr. *Dialogue with Trypho*. In *The Ante-Nicene Fathers*, vol. 1. Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 1885. The earliest sustained Christian exegetical engagement with Judaism on messianic prophecy.

Bock, Darrell L., and Mitch Glaser, eds. *The Gospel According to Isaiah 53: Encountering the Suffering Servant in Jewish and Christian Theology*. Grand Rapids: Kregel, 2012. Evangelical exegetical and apologetic treatment of Isaiah 53; includes engagement with Jewish readings.

Motyer, J. Alec. *The Prophecy of Isaiah: An Introduction and Commentary*. Downers Grove: IVP, 1993. Detailed exegetical commentary arguing for an individual Servant reading.

Oswalt, John N. *The Book of Isaiah: Chapters 40-66*. New International Commentary on the Old Testament. Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 1998. Standard evangelical commentary on Second Isaiah; thorough treatment of the Servant Songs.

Kaiser, Walter C. *The Messiah in the Old Testament*. Grand Rapids: Zondervan, 1995. Surveys messianic texts including Isaiah 53, Daniel 9, and Psalm 22 with attention to original context.

Anderson, Robert. *The Coming Prince*. London: Hodder & Stoughton, 1894. The classic evangelical calculation of Daniel's seventy weeks using the Artaxerxes decree and 360-day prophetic years.

Hoehner, Harold. *Chronological Aspects of the Life of Christ*. Grand Rapids: Zondervan, 1977. More recent recalculation of the Daniel 9 chronology using astronomical data to fix the Artaxerxes decree.

Hengel, Martin. *The Atonement: The Origins of the Doctrine in the New Testament*. London: SCM, 1981. On the use of Isaiah 53 in early Christian theology of the cross.

Jeremias, Joachim. "pais theou." In *Theological Dictionary of the New Testament*, vol. 5. Translated and edited by G. W. Bromiley. Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 1967. On the Servant / pais traditions in Jewish and early Christian thought.

## Comparative and cross-tradition works

Hagner, Donald A. *The Jewish Reclamation of Jesus: An Analysis and Critique of Modern Jewish Study of Jesus*. Grand Rapids: Zondervan, 1984. Survey of modern Jewish engagement with Jesus as a historical figure, distinct from the messianic question.

Skarsaune, Oskar. *In the Shadow of the Temple: Jewish Influences on Early Christianity*. Downers Grove: IVP, 2002. On how Jewish interpretive traditions shaped early Christian reading of messianic texts.

Neusner, Jacob, and Bruce D. Chilton, eds. *The Brother of Jesus: James the Just and His Mission*. Louisville: Westminster John Knox, 2001. Includes discussion of early Jewish-Christian dialogue and the messianic question in the first century.

Patai, Raphael. *The Messiah Texts*. Detroit: Wayne State University Press, 1979. Comprehensive anthology of Jewish messianic texts across periods; essential for understanding the range of Jewish messianic expectation.

Levine, Amy-Jill. *The Misunderstood Jew: The Church and the Scandal of the Jewish Jesus*. New York: HarperOne, 2006. A Jewish New Testament scholar's account of how Christianity has misread both Jesus and the prophetic texts in its messianic arguments.

Charlesworth, James H., ed. *The Messiah: Developments in Earliest Judaism and Christianity*. Minneapolis: Fortress, 1992. Papers from a major scholarly colloquium on Second Temple messianic concepts; includes detailed treatment of Isaiah 53 and Daniel 9 in pre-Christian Jewish context.

Evans, Craig A. *Jesus and His Contemporaries: Comparative Studies*. Leiden: Brill, 1995. On the historical Jesus in his Jewish context, including his use of prophetic and psalmic texts.

Segal, Alan F. *Two Powers in Heaven: Early Rabbinic Reports about Christianity and Gnosticism*. Leiden: Brill, 1977. On the exegetical traditions behind early Jewish and Christian claims about divine agency, relevant to the Servant's identity.
