## Domain & Evidence

Both positions affirm that humans are created by God, that the physical body is good and not a prison, that bodily resurrection is the ultimate eschatological hope rather than a purely spiritual afterlife, and that Scripture rather than Greek philosophy should govern anthropological conclusions.

Both employ grammatical-historical hermeneutics in principle. Monists treat Hebrew anthropological vocabulary as evidence that the biblical writers conceived of the person as a unified psychosomatic whole, treating dichotomist and trichotomist readings as imports from Platonic or Cartesian categories. Dualists treat the same vocabulary alongside specific New Testament texts as evidence that the person has both a material and an immaterial constituent, with the immaterial capable of existing apart from the body in the intermediate state.

This document maps each side's strongest case, their critiques of the opposing position, and the live hermeneutical and philosophical disputes, without forcing resolution.

The debate intersects with three other loci. Eschatologically, it bears directly on the nature of the intermediate state between death and resurrection and, at one remove, on conditionalist versus ECT accounts of final judgment. Epistemologically, it intersects with neuroscience and philosophy of mind. Historically, it traces the patristic reception of Platonic soul-body categories and asks whether that reception distorted the biblical witness.

The live disagreements center on a set of identifiable questions: what Hebrew terms like *nephesh*, *ruach*, and *neshamah* actually denote ontologically, whether the New Testament introduces a distinct immaterial substance category that Hebrew anthropology did not require, what the intermediate state texts (2 Corinthians 5:1-10; Philippians 1:21-23; Revelation 6:9-11) require ontologically, and whether physicalism or monism can adequately account for personal identity through death and resurrection.

## Key Definitions

### Monism / Physicalism / Holism

**Position A, monism:** the human person is a unified psychosomatic whole; there is no immaterial substance (soul or spirit) capable of independent existence apart from the body. Death is the cessation of the whole person. Resurrection is the re-creation or restoration of the whole person by God. The terms *nephesh*, *ruach*, and *neshamah* describe functional aspects of the embodied person, not separable substances.

**Position B, dualism:** monism cannot account for the intermediate state, personal identity through death, or the New Testament's language of departure and being with Christ, and must either deny those texts or interpret them in ways that strain their plain sense.

### Substance Dualism

**Position B, substance dualism:** the human person consists of two ontologically distinct substances, a material body and an immaterial soul or spirit; the soul is the seat of personal identity and is capable of existing in a disembodied state between death and resurrection.

**Position A, monism:** classical substance dualism, especially in its Cartesian form, is not the biblical view; it privileges the immaterial over the material and creates an interaction problem that has no satisfying solution, and it reads Greek philosophical categories into Hebrew texts that do not support them.

### Holistic Dualism

**Position B (qualified):** a mediating position that holds the human person is a genuine psychosomatic unity in normal existence but that the immaterial aspect of the person can survive bodily death in an attenuated, incomplete state, awaiting reunion with the body at resurrection. John Cooper's formulation is representative.

**Position A:** holistic dualism is an attempt to retain the soul's survival while acknowledging the body's importance, but the ontological mechanism for the soul's survival is not clearly grounded in the biblical text and relies on philosophical construction more than exegesis.

### Nephesh

**Position A:** *nephesh* in the Hebrew Bible most often means a living being, a person, or a vital self; it is used of animals as well as humans (Genesis 1:20, 24); it does not denote an immaterial substance distinct from the body but rather the living animated creature as a whole.

**Position B:** *nephesh* can refer to the animating principle that gives life to the body; in some texts it approaches the sense of an inner self that survives death (Psalm 16:10; 49:15), which points toward at least a functional distinction between the person and the purely physical body.

### The Intermediate State

**Position A:** the intermediate state, if it exists as conscious experience, does not require a substance-dualist ontology; some monists accept a form of immediate resurrection or conditional existence sustained entirely by divine action; others accept soul sleep as consistent with monism.

**Position B:** an intermediate state in which the person is consciously present with Christ between death and resurrection (Philippians 1:23; 2 Corinthians 5:8; Luke 23:43) requires that something of the person survives bodily death, which requires at minimum a functional distinction between the immaterial self and the physical body.

## Foundational Assumptions

### Position A assumes

Monists begin with **Hebrew holism as the controlling anthropological frame**. The Old Testament does not have a developed doctrine of an immaterial soul distinct from the body; persons are animated bodies, not embodied souls. If the New Testament introduces a different ontology, it must do so clearly and explicitly, not merely by using Greek vocabulary that happens to overlap with Platonic categories.

A further assumption is that **resurrection, not soul survival, is the biblical answer to death**. If survival of an immaterial soul were the primary mechanism of continuity through death, resurrection would be redundant or merely cosmetic. The fact that resurrection is the central eschatological hope indicates that the whole person dies and the whole person is raised, which fits monism rather than dualism.

### Position B assumes

Dualists begin with **the two-stage eschatology implied by the New Testament's intermediate state texts**. Paul expects to depart and be with Christ (Philippians 1:23), which is better than remaining alive (v. 21-22), meaning the state between death and resurrection is not non-existence but conscious fellowship. That intermediate state requires that something of the person persists between bodily death and bodily resurrection.

A further assumption is that **personal identity through death requires ontological continuity**. If the person wholly ceases to exist at death, the resurrected person is a new creation with implanted memories rather than the same person raised. The resurrected person's identity with the pre-death person requires some thread of continuity through the death event, and a surviving immaterial self provides that thread more naturally than a re-creation from nothing.

# Position A: Monism / Holism: Strongest Case

---

## Argument 1: Hebrew Anthropology Is Holistic, Not Partitive

The primary Hebrew anthropological terms do not carve the person into separable substances. *Nephesh* (755 occurrences in the Hebrew Bible) refers to a living being, a person, a throat, a desire, a life, never to an immaterial substance separable from the body. When God forms the man from dust and breathes into his nostrils, the man *becomes* a *nephesh hayyah* (Genesis 2:7), a living creature, rather than receiving a soul as a separate component. *Ruach* (breath, wind, spirit) and *neshamah* (breath of life) describe animating energy given by God rather than a distinct ontological substance. This pattern is consistent throughout the Hebrew canon, and the absence of a developed intermediate state doctrine in most of the Old Testament fits the pattern: if persons are psychosomatic wholes, death is the cessation of the whole, and hope is in resurrection rather than soul survival.

> **P1:** Hebrew anthropological terms (*nephesh*, *ruach*, *neshamah*) describe functional aspects of the animated person rather than separable immaterial substances.
>
> **P2:** Genesis 2:7 presents the person as becoming a living being upon receiving the breath of life, not as receiving a soul as a distinct component added to a pre-existing body.
>
> **P3:** The minimal development of intermediate state doctrine in the Hebrew canon is consistent with a holistic anthropology in which death affects the whole person.
>
> **C:** The Old Testament's default anthropological frame is holistic, and readings that import a substance-dualist ontology into Hebrew vocabulary impose a category the texts do not carry.

## Argument 2: The Body Matters Ontologically, Not Just Functionally

If the biblical view were substance dualism, the physical body would be an instrument or vehicle for the soul, not constitutive of the person. But the New Testament treats the body as integral to personal identity in ways that resist that reading. Paul calls the body the temple of the Holy Spirit (1 Corinthians 6:19-20) and argues that what happens to the body matters morally and spiritually. The resurrection is of the *same* body, transformed, but in genuine continuity with the earthly body (1 Corinthians 15:42-44; Romans 8:11). If the soul were the real person and the body merely its vehicle, the resurrection body's continuity with the earthly body would be theologically incidental. Its significance in the New Testament points to the body as constitutive of the person rather than separable from it.

> **P1:** 1 Corinthians 6:19-20 treats the body as the temple of the Spirit and the site of moral accountability, not as an expendable vehicle.
>
> **P2:** 1 Corinthians 15 and Romans 8:11 present resurrection as involving the same body transformed, implying that bodily continuity is theologically significant rather than incidental.
>
> **P3:** If the soul were the seat of personal identity and the body merely instrumental, bodily resurrection would add nothing that soul survival alone does not already provide.
>
> **C:** The New Testament's insistence on bodily resurrection as the eschatological goal, rather than disembodied soul survival, implies that the body is constitutive of the person, consistent with monism.

## Argument 3: Resurrection as the Exclusive Hope Implies Whole-Person Death

Across both Testaments, the primary answer to death is resurrection, not immortality of the soul. Martha's confession in John 11:24 is "I know that he will rise again in the resurrection on the last day", the natural Jewish expectation. Paul's argument in 1 Corinthians 15 treats the resurrection of Christ as the firstfruits of the general resurrection; if there were already a robust soul-immortality that guaranteed personal continuity through death, the theological weight Paul places on the resurrection would be difficult to explain. The Epicureans and other Greeks already had accounts of soul survival; what distinguished Christian proclamation was bodily resurrection. That distinction makes more sense if death is a whole-person event requiring a whole-person reversal.

> **P1:** Martha's expectation (John 11:24) and the general Jewish eschatological framework ground hope in resurrection rather than soul survival.
>
> **P2:** Paul's argument in 1 Corinthians 15 places the entire weight of Christian hope on the resurrection; if soul survival guaranteed continuity, the resurrection's stakes would be lower than Paul indicates.
>
> **P3:** The distinctiveness of Christian proclamation against Greco-Roman backgrounds was bodily resurrection, not the immortality of the soul, which suggests the two are not interchangeable.
>
> **C:** Resurrection as the exclusive and weighty eschatological hope fits better with a whole-person death that requires a whole-person restoration than with a dualism in which the soul survives independently.

## Argument 4: Neuroscience and the Mind-Brain Dependence Argument

Every mental state, including memory, personality, emotion, and consciousness, has a demonstrable neurological correlate. Damage to the brain alters or destroys personality and cognition in ways that suggest the mind is not an independent substance operating through the brain but is constituted by the brain's physical processes. While this does not prove monism by itself, it creates a strong evidential presumption against substance dualism that any dualist anthropology must address. Christian physicalists argue that this evidence aligns with a biblical holism in which persons are embodied creatures whose mental and spiritual life is mediated through physical processes, rather than with a view in which the soul operates independently of the body.

> **P1:** Neuroscientific evidence demonstrates tight correlations between brain states and mental states, including damage patterns that alter personality and memory in predictable ways.
>
> **P2:** Substance dualism requires that the soul operates through but is ontologically independent of the brain, which makes the tight mind-brain dependence difficult to explain without ad hoc mechanisms.
>
> **P3:** A monist anthropology in which mental life is constituted by physical processes aligns naturally with the neuroscientific evidence without requiring an interaction mechanism.
>
> **C:** The neuroscientific evidence creates a cumulative presumption in favor of monism that the dualist must account for with independent grounds.

## Position A: Critique of Position B

### Critique 1: Substance Dualism Is a Greek Import, Not a Biblical Doctrine

The classic substance dualist view of the soul as an immaterial, naturally immortal substance distinct from the body derives from Plato's *Phaedo* and *Republic*, not from the Hebrew Bible. The early church absorbed this framework through its engagement with Middle Platonism, and patristic writers including Origen used it to interpret both the intermediate state and the resurrection. The subsequent theological tradition inherited this Platonic framework as if it were the biblical view. The monist case begins by stripping that assumption and asking what the biblical texts themselves require, without the Platonic grid.

> **P1:** Plato's *Phaedo* articulates the immaterial, naturally immortal soul as a distinct substance; this framework is absent from the Hebrew Bible.
>
> **P2:** The early church's engagement with Middle Platonism introduced soul-immortality categories into Christian anthropology as a philosophical import.
>
> **P3:** The subsequent theological tradition treated the Platonic soul as the biblical view, conflating a philosophical category with a biblical exegetical conclusion.
>
> **C:** Substance dualism is a historically traceable Platonic import into Christian theology; the biblical texts do not require it and in many cases sit uncomfortably with it.

### Critique 2: The Intermediate State Texts Do Not Require Substance Dualism

Dualists rely heavily on Philippians 1:21-23 and 2 Corinthians 5:1-10 to establish a conscious intermediate existence. Monists respond that these texts establish that being with Christ is the believer's immediate experience following death, but they do not specify the ontological mechanism by which that experience occurs. God's power to sustain personal existence through death does not require a naturally immortal immaterial substance; it requires only divine action. Immediate resurrection, conditional consciousness sustained by God, or other models can account for the experiential data of these texts without committing to substance dualism.

> **P1:** Philippians 1:23 and 2 Corinthians 5:8 establish that the believer's post-death experience is conscious fellowship with Christ.
>
> **P2:** The ontological mechanism by which that experience occurs is not specified in those texts; they describe the experience, not its metaphysical substrate.
>
> **P3:** Divine action sustaining personal existence through death is a sufficient mechanism; a naturally immortal immaterial substance is not required.
>
> **C:** The intermediate state texts establish conscious post-death existence but do not require the substance-dualist ontology dualists read into them.

### Critique 3: Dualism Creates a Two-Tier Person With Gnostic-Adjacent Problems

Substance dualism tends to privilege the immaterial soul as the real person and to treat the body as secondary or instrumental. Even when dualists resist this tendency, the ontological distinction between soul and body creates a structural pressure toward it. This pressure has historically produced ascetic traditions that devalue the body, spiritualized accounts of salvation that neglect material justice and creation care, and popular Christian views of heaven as a purely spiritual state that the resurrection corrects only incidentally. Monism avoids this by treating the bodily, material, and social dimensions of human existence as constitutive of personhood rather than as its context.

> **P1:** Substance dualism's ontological distinction between soul and body creates structural pressure to treat the soul as the real person and the body as secondary.
>
> **P2:** This pressure has historically generated body-devaluing practices and spiritualized soteriology that the New Testament's material eschatology resists.
>
> **P3:** Monism treats the body as constitutive of personhood, which aligns with the biblical emphasis on bodily resurrection, creation care, and material justice.
>
> **C:** Substance dualism's structural tendency to devalue the body conflicts with the trajectory of biblical eschatology; monism avoids this structural problem by design.

## Position A: Rebuttals to Position B

### Rebuttal 1: The Personal Identity Problem Revisited

Dualists argue that monism cannot preserve personal identity through death because, if the whole person ceases to exist, the resurrected person is a numerically distinct new creation. Monists respond that personal identity through resurrection is grounded in God's knowledge and sovereign act rather than in the continuity of a surviving substance. God who knows every person exhaustively can resurrect the same person without requiring a metaphysical thread of immaterial substance to bridge the gap; divine identification is a sufficient ground for numerical identity. This is no more problematic than asking how God creates personal identity at conception.

> **P1:** God's exhaustive knowledge of every person provides a sufficient basis for identifying the resurrected person with the pre-death person.
>
> **P2:** Divine action establishing numerical identity does not require a surviving immaterial substance any more than it requires one at creation.
>
> **P3:** The personal identity problem is a philosophical challenge for any view of resurrection; it does not uniquely favor dualism over monism.
>
> **C:** Divine identification grounded in God's omniscience provides a sufficient account of personal identity through death without requiring substance dualism.

### Rebuttal 2: Matthew 10:28 Revisited

Dualists cite Matthew 10:28, "Do not fear those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul," as direct evidence for a soul distinct from and surviving the body. Monists respond that *psyche* in this context can be read as the whole person or the true self, the life that men cannot ultimately destroy, rather than as a Platonic immaterial substance. The contrast is between what human enemies can do (kill the body) and what they cannot ultimately do (destroy the person's true existence in God), not a metaphysical statement about two separable ontological substances.

> **P1:** *Psyche* in the New Testament does not uniformly denote an immaterial substance; it can mean life, self, or person as a whole.
>
> **P2:** Matthew 10:28's contrast is between human power (limited to the body) and God's power (which determines the person's ultimate existence), a soteriological point rather than a metaphysical anatomy.
>
> **P3:** Reading a Platonic substance ontology into a passage whose point is about where ultimate threat and ultimate security lie is a category import.
>
> **C:** Matthew 10:28 makes a point about the limits of human power over the believer's ultimate existence, not a claim about the ontology of an immaterial soul substance.

### Rebuttal 3: The Resurrection Redundancy Problem Revisited

Dualists argue that monism makes resurrection the exclusive hope, which is correct and intended. But they sometimes argue that this makes the intermediate state incoherent for monists. Monists respond that conditional existence, immediate resurrection, or soul sleep are all coherent options that account for the experiential data without requiring a naturally immortal substance. Paul's own language in 1 Corinthians 15:35-54 describes resurrection as transformation of the body from a perishable to an imperishable state, which is a whole-person event; it does not describe the reunion of a soul with a body after a period of disembodied existence.

> **P1:** 1 Corinthians 15:35-54 describes resurrection as the transformation of the whole person from perishable to imperishable, not as the soul's reunion with a body.
>
> **P2:** Monist accounts of the intermediate state (conditional existence, immediate resurrection, soul sleep) preserve the intermediate state experience without requiring substance dualism.
>
> **P3:** The choice is not between dualism and the denial of any intermediate existence; it is between dualism and monist alternatives that achieve the same experiential result by different mechanisms.
>
> **C:** Monism does not require the denial of intermediate conscious existence; it requires only that the ontological mechanism for that existence be divine action rather than natural soul immortality.

# Position B: Dualism / Holistic Dualism: Strongest Case

---

## Argument 1: The Intermediate State Requires Ontological Distinction

The New Testament contains multiple texts that presuppose conscious personal existence between bodily death and bodily resurrection. Paul writes that "to depart and be with Christ" is "far better" than remaining alive (Philippians 1:21-23); the comparison only makes sense if departure is not annihilation or unconscious sleep but a positive relational state. In 2 Corinthians 5:6-8, Paul contrasts being "at home in the body" with being "away from the body and at home with the Lord," again presupposing that the person can exist away from the body. In Revelation 6:9-11, the souls of martyrs under the altar are conscious, communicating, and awaiting the resurrection. These texts collectively require that something of the person persists between bodily death and resurrection, and that something is what dualists identify as the soul.

> **P1:** Philippians 1:23 presents departure as consciously preferable to continued embodied life, which requires that departure is a positive state, not non-existence.
>
> **P2:** 2 Corinthians 5:6-8 explicitly contrasts being at home in the body with being away from the body and at home with the Lord, presupposing that the person can exist in a disembodied state.
>
> **P3:** Revelation 6:9-11 depicts souls in a conscious intermediate state, communicating and awaiting the resurrection.
>
> **C:** The New Testament intermediate state texts require that something of the person persists between bodily death and resurrection, which is what substance or holistic dualism identifies as the immaterial soul.

## Argument 2: The Language of Departure and Return Implies Two-Stage Eschatology

Paul's eschatological framework in 1 Thessalonians 4:13-17 describes the dead in Christ as those whose spirits God will bring with Christ at the Parousia, after which they receive resurrection bodies. The sequence is: (1) death, (2) the soul/spirit with Christ in the intermediate state, (3) resurrection reunion of spirit and body at the Parousia. This two-stage structure, which John Cooper calls the "two-stage eschatology," is structurally incompatible with monism, which can at best offer immediate resurrection or soul sleep but cannot account for the Pauline sequence in which the spirit precedes the resurrection body at the Parousia.

> **P1:** 1 Thessalonians 4:14 states that God will bring with Jesus "those who have fallen asleep," implying the dead are already with him before the resurrection.
>
> **P2:** Verse 16 then speaks of the dead in Christ rising, indicating a resurrection event subsequent to their already being with Christ.
>
> **P3:** The sequence, being with Christ then receiving resurrection body, is a two-stage structure that requires personal existence in the intermediate period.
>
> **C:** The Pauline two-stage eschatology in 1 Thessalonians 4 requires a surviving personal constituent in the intermediate state, consistent with dualism and inconsistent with whole-person cessation at death.

## Argument 3: Matthew 10:28 and the Body-Soul Distinction

Jesus in Matthew 10:28 explicitly distinguishes between those who kill the body (*soma*) and cannot kill the soul (*psyche*) and God who can destroy both soul and body in Gehenna. The distinction between body and soul as two components that can be separately destroyed or preserved is a direct statement of anthropological dualism in the teaching of Jesus. The soul is not coextensive with the body; it is a distinct entity that human agents cannot reach by killing the body. This is the plainest possible statement of the kind of ontological distinction that dualism requires.

> **P1:** Jesus distinguishes between the body (*soma*) and the soul (*psyche*) as components that human agents and God can affect differently.
>
> **P2:** Human agents can kill the body but not the soul; God can destroy both, a distinction that requires the soul to be capable of existing apart from the body.
>
> **P3:** The passage is not about soteriological security alone; it makes an implicit ontological claim about what human killing can and cannot reach.
>
> **C:** Matthew 10:28 directly supports the view that the person has an immaterial constituent that survives bodily death, consistent with dualism.

## Argument 4: Personal Identity Through Death Requires Ontological Continuity

Resurrection as the New Testament presents it is not the creation of a new person but the raising of the same person. Paul refers to the resurrection body as the same body that is sown and raised (1 Corinthians 15:42-44), the continuity language is intentional. For the raised person to be numerically identical to the pre-death person, there must be some thread of continuity through the death event. Divine re-creation of a person who shares memories and characteristics is not resurrection of the same person; it is creation of a replica. A surviving immaterial soul provides the metaphysical thread of numerical identity that makes resurrection, rather than new creation, the right description of what occurs.

> **P1:** The New Testament presents resurrection as the raising of the same person, not the creation of a new person with identical characteristics.
>
> **P2:** Numerical identity through a period of non-existence is philosophically contested; a surviving immaterial self provides a cleaner account of personal continuity.
>
> **P3:** Without some thread of continuity through death, what occurs at the resurrection is better described as new creation than as the raising of the same person.
>
> **C:** Personal identity through death, as required by the New Testament's resurrection language, is more naturally accounted for by a surviving immaterial self than by monist alternatives.

## Position B: Critique of Position A

### Critique 1: Monism Cannot Preserve Conscious Intermediate Existence

The monist is forced to choose between denying conscious intermediate existence (soul sleep, whole-person death) and positing mechanisms (immediate resurrection, conditional consciousness) for which there is no clear biblical warrant. Soul sleep conflicts with Philippians 1:23, which frames departure as immediately preferable, not as a suspended state to be followed eventually by something preferable. Immediate resurrection introduces a category not clearly present in the New Testament's sequential eschatology. The monist's alternatives are all reconstructions; dualism reads the intermediate state texts more straightforwardly.

> **P1:** Soul sleep conflicts with Philippians 1:23's framing of departure as immediately better than continued life.
>
> **P2:** Immediate resurrection introduces a sequential eschatology foreign to 1 Thessalonians 4's structure, which presents the dead receiving resurrection bodies at the Parousia rather than at death.
>
> **P3:** Monist alternatives to the intermediate state require philosophical construction rather than direct textual warrant.
>
> **C:** Monism cannot account for the conscious intermediate state the New Testament describes without mechanisms that require more philosophical construction than the dualist reading.

### Critique 2: Hebrew Holism Does Not Exclude Functional Dualism

Monists argue that Hebrew anthropology is holistic and therefore non-dualist. Dualists respond that holism and dualism are not mutually exclusive: a person can be a genuine psychosomatic unity in normal existence while still having an immaterial constituent capable of surviving bodily death in an attenuated state. Cooper's "holistic dualism" accepts the Hebrew emphasis on whole-person unity without conceding that the immaterial aspect of the person cannot exist apart from the body. The holism shows up in normal existence and in the resurrection hope; it does not settle the question of what happens in the abnormal, temporary state of bodily death.

> **P1:** Holism describes the normal integrated functioning of body and soul; it does not entail that no immaterial constituent exists capable of surviving in a reduced state.
>
> **P2:** Cooper's holistic dualism accepts the Hebrew psychosomatic unity while maintaining the soul's capacity for temporary disembodied existence.
>
> **P3:** The question of what survives bodily death is separate from the question of how body and soul normally function together.
>
> **C:** Hebrew holism is compatible with a dualism that affirms the soul's capacity for temporary disembodied existence; the two positions address different aspects of the same question.

### Critique 3: Divine Re-Creation Is Not Resurrection

Monists argue that God's omniscience is a sufficient basis for resurrecting the same person after whole-person death. Dualists respond that this move covertly substitutes divine re-creation for resurrection. If the person wholly ceases to exist and God creates a new person who is qualitatively identical, the right description is that God made a perfect copy, not that he raised the same person. The New Testament's resurrection language requires numerical identity, not merely qualitative similarity. Without a surviving thread of personal existence through death, the resurrection claim loses the specific content that distinguishes it from new creation.

> **P1:** Numerical identity requires more than qualitative similarity; an identical copy is not the same person.
>
> **P2:** God's knowledge of a person who no longer exists is knowledge of a past entity; using that knowledge to create a new entity is creation, not resurrection.
>
> **P3:** The New Testament's resurrection language (raising the same person) implies numerical identity, which requires more than divine creative power applied to a blueprint.
>
> **C:** Monism's appeal to divine omniscience as a ground for resurrection identity conflates qualitative re-creation with the numerical identity that resurrection requires.

## Position B: Rebuttals to Position A

### Rebuttal 1: The Greek Import Charge Revisited

Monists argue that substance dualism is a Platonic import. Dualists respond that the rejection of substance dualism on the grounds that it is Greek is itself a genetic fallacy: the origin of an idea does not determine its truth or its scriptural compatibility. Furthermore, the New Testament was written in Greek by authors immersed in a Hellenistic world, and some degree of conceptual engagement with that world is not a disqualifier. The question is whether the New Testament texts require a body-soul distinction, not whether the vocabulary for it came from Hebrew or Greek sources. Cooper argues the New Testament does require it on exegetical grounds independent of Platonic provenance.

> **P1:** The origin of a concept in Greek philosophy does not by itself render it unbiblical; what matters is whether the biblical texts teach or require it.
>
> **P2:** The New Testament authors wrote in Greek within a Hellenistic context; some conceptual overlap with Greek categories is neither surprising nor disqualifying.
>
> **P3:** Cooper's exegetical case for holistic dualism is based on what the intermediate state texts require, not on the claim that Plato had the right anthropology.
>
> **C:** The Greek import charge is a genetic argument that does not address the exegetical case for dualism; the question must be settled by the texts themselves.

### Rebuttal 2: The Neuroscience Argument Revisited

Dualists accept that mind-brain correlations are tight and real but argue that correlation does not establish identity. The fact that brain damage affects the soul's operations does not prove the soul is identical to the brain any more than damage to a radio proves that the broadcast originates in the radio. The soul may operate through the brain, and damage to the brain may impair the soul's access to and expression through the body, without the soul being reducible to the brain. This is the standard dualist response to the neuroscience argument, and it is not obviously wrong; the inference from tight correlation to identity requires additional philosophical premises that are contested.

> **P1:** Correlation between brain states and mental states does not entail identity; correlation is consistent with causal dependence without ontological reduction.
>
> **P2:** A dualist can hold that the soul operates through the brain and is affected by brain damage without holding that the soul is identical to or constituted by the brain.
>
> **P3:** The inference from tight mind-brain correlation to monism requires philosophical premises (that causal dependence implies identity) that are themselves contested in philosophy of mind.
>
> **C:** The neuroscience argument does not establish monism; it establishes mind-brain correlation, which is compatible with dualism given the right account of how soul and brain interact.

### Rebuttal 3: Resurrection Redundancy Revisited

Monists argue that the theological weight Paul places on resurrection implies whole-person death, which is correct and intended. But they sometimes argue that this makes the intermediate state incoherent for monists. Dualists respond that resurrection retains full theological weight even on dualism: the intermediate state is explicitly described by Paul as "naked" and incomplete (2 Corinthians 5:3-4), a state he does not desire as a permanent condition. Resurrection reunites the person with a transformed body, completing the person's existence in a way that disembodied soul survival cannot. Dualism does not make resurrection cosmetic; it makes it the culmination of the person's existence after a temporary and impoverished intermediate period.

> **P1:** Paul describes the disembodied intermediate state as "naked" and undesired as a permanent condition (2 Corinthians 5:3-4), indicating it is incomplete rather than final.
>
> **P2:** Resurrection on dualism is the completion and transformation of the whole person, reuniting soul with a glorified body; it is not theologically redundant.
>
> **P3:** The distinction between a temporary attenuated intermediate state and the full eschatological existence of resurrection preserves the theological weight Paul places on the resurrection event.
>
> **C:** Dualism does not make resurrection redundant; the incomplete intermediate state and the completing resurrection event are structurally distinct, preserving the resurrection's full theological significance.

# Connection to Conditional Immortality

This debate connects directly to the ECT vs. Conditional Immortality question, but the connection is not a strict logical entailment in either direction.

Monism fits most naturally with conditional immortality and annihilationism: if persons are psychosomatic wholes who die wholly at death and are raised only by divine action, then immortality is not a natural property of the soul but a gift of God given to the redeemed at resurrection. The wicked are not raised to an immortal existence of torment; they are raised, judged, and cease to exist. The anthropological and eschatological positions are mutually reinforcing.

However, the entailment does not run cleanly in both directions. Some conditionalists hold a form of holistic dualism and affirm a conscious intermediate state; their conditionalism is grounded in the judgment outcome at resurrection rather than in monist anthropology. Conversely, some monists are universalists, and some are ECT proponents who hold that God sustains the wicked in existence for eternal conscious torment without appealing to natural soul immortality.

The cleanest connection is between **Platonic substance dualism** and **ECT**: if the soul is naturally immortal and incapable of annihilation, ECT follows almost automatically, since the soul cannot simply cease. Break the natural immortality assumption and ECT requires additional positive warrant.

See also: `theology/Eschatology/Eternal Conscious Torment vs Annihilation.md`

# The Three Actual Hinges

The real issue is not whether the body matters or whether resurrection is central. Both positions affirm both. The real issue is **whether the person has an immaterial constituent capable of existing apart from the body**, and specifically whether that is required by the intermediate state texts or merely consistent with them. Monists argue the texts require only that the person is consciously with Christ after death, not that a specific immaterial substance accomplishes that; dualists argue the texts require at minimum a surviving personal constituent that the monist cannot provide without philosophical construction.

A second hinge is **personal identity through death**. Dualists argue a surviving soul provides the metaphysical thread of numerical identity that resurrection requires. Monists argue divine identification is a sufficient ground, making the soul unnecessary for identity. The dispute is partly a philosophical one about what numerical identity through time requires, not purely an exegetical one.

A third hinge is **the relationship between Hebrew holism and ontological dualism**. Monists treat Hebrew holism as evidence against any body-soul distinction. Dualists treat holism as a description of the person's normal psychosomatic integration that is compatible with a distinct immaterial constituent capable of temporary disembodied existence. The two sides are partly talking past each other: holism addresses normal functioning, while dualism addresses what survives an abnormal event (death). Clarifying which question is being answered eliminates a significant portion of the apparent disagreement.

## Monist / Physicalist

Brown, Warren S., Nancey Murphy, and H. Newton Malony, eds. *Whatever Happened to the Soul? Scientific and Theological Portraits of Human Nature*. Minneapolis: Fortress Press, 1998. Multi-author case for non-reductive physicalism drawing on neuroscience, philosophy, and biblical studies.

Corcoran, Kevin. *Rethinking Human Nature: A Christian Materialist Alternative to the Soul*. Grand Rapids: Baker Academic, 2006. Defends a constitution-view physicalism compatible with resurrection and personal identity.

Green, Joel B. *Body, Soul, and Human Life: The Nature of Humanity in the Bible*. Studies in Theological Interpretation. Grand Rapids: Baker Academic, 2008. Exegetical case for holistic anthropology across both Testaments; engagement with neuroscience.

Green, Joel B., ed. *What About the Soul? Neuroscience and Christian Anthropology*. Nashville: Abingdon Press, 2004. Essays integrating neuroscientific findings with a non-dualist theological anthropology.

Johnston, Philip S. *Shades of Sheol: Death and Afterlife in the Old Testament*. Downers Grove, IL: InterVarsity Press, 2002. Careful OT exegesis of death, Sheol, and afterlife; supports the minimal development of soul-survival in the Hebrew canon.

Murphy, Nancey. *Bodies and Souls, or Spirited Bodies?* Current Issues in Theology. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2006. Philosophical and theological defense of non-reductive physicalism; engages the free will and moral responsibility implications.

Murphy, Nancey, and Warren S. Brown. *Did My Neurons Make Me Do It? Philosophical and Neurobiological Perspectives on Moral Responsibility and Free Will*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2007. Extends the physicalist anthropology to agency and accountability.

Stendahl, Krister. "Immortality Is Too Much and Too Little." In *Meanings: The Bible as Document and as Guide*, 193–202. Philadelphia: Fortress Press, 1984. Influential essay arguing that the Greek immortality-of-the-soul concept distorts the biblical resurrection hope.

Wolff, Hans Walter. *Anthropology of the Old Testament*. Translated by Margaret Kohl. Philadelphia: Fortress, 1974. Standard lexical study of Hebrew anthropological terms; foundational for the holistic reading of *nephesh*, *ruach*, and *basar*.

Wright, N. T. *The Resurrection of the Son of God*. Christian Origins and the Question of God 3. Minneapolis: Fortress, 2003. Comprehensive historical and exegetical argument that resurrection, not soul-immortality, is the driving eschatological category in the New Testament.

Wright, N. T. "Mind, Spirit, Soul, and Body: All for One and One for All, Reflections on Paul's Anthropology in His Complex Contexts." Society of Christian Philosophers, Fordham University, March 2011. https://ntwrightpage.com/2016/07/12/mind-spirit-soul-and-body/. Accessible essay on Paul's anthropological vocabulary; resists both crude dualism and reductive physicalism.

## Dualist / Holistic Dualist

Berkhof, Louis. *Systematic Theology*. Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 1941. Classic Reformed systematic; treatment of the dichotomous nature of man and the intermediate state represents the standard conservative dualist position.

Cooper, John W. *Body, Soul, and Life Everlasting: Biblical Anthropology and the Monism-Dualism Debate*. Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 1989. 2nd ed. 2000. The fullest exegetical and philosophical case for holistic dualism; direct engagement with monist alternatives on the intermediate state.

Gundry, Robert H. *Sōma in Biblical Theology: With Emphasis on Pauline Anthropology*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1976. Detailed study of *soma* in Paul arguing for a consistent body-soul distinction in Pauline anthropology.

Hasker, William. *The Emergent Self*. Ithaca, NY: Cornell University Press, 1999. Philosophical defense of emergent dualism; the soul as a genuinely distinct entity that emerges from but is not reducible to the brain.

Moreland, J. P., and Scott B. Rae. *Body and Soul: Human Nature and the Crisis in Ethics*. Downers Grove, IL: InterVarsity Press, 2000. Philosophical and ethical case for substance dualism; argues that moral and personal identity claims require an immaterial soul.

Moreland, J. P. *The Soul: How We Know It's Real and Why It Matters*. Chicago: Moody Publishers, 2014. Popular-level but philosophically informed defense of soul-body dualism against physicalist alternatives.

Plantinga, Alvin. "Against Materialism." *Faith and Philosophy* 23, no. 1 (2006): 3–32. Rigorous philosophical argument that materialism about persons is incoherent; influential in Christian analytic philosophy.

Swinburne, Richard. *The Evolution of the Soul*. Rev. ed. Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997. Systematic philosophical defense of substance dualism; argues that mental events are not identical to brain events and that a soul survives death.

Talbot, Mark R. "True Psychology Serves Theology." In *Christian Perspectives on Being Human*, edited by J. P. Moreland and David M. Ciocchi, 195–233. Grand Rapids: Baker, 1993. Argues that a robust Christian psychology requires anthropological dualism.
