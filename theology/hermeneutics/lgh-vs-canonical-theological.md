# Part One: Debate Outline

---

## Domain & Evidence

This debate is methodological before it is doctrinal. The dispute is not over whether Scripture is authoritative, but over how its meaning is identified and how later biblical texts may legitimately use earlier ones. The literal-grammatical-historical approach treats the original author's intended meaning, as expressed in the grammar, genre, and historical setting of the text, as the controlling norm for interpretation. The canonical-theological approach argues that Scripture, because it has a divine author and a unified redemptive structure, may carry significance that exceeds what the human author consciously grasped at the time of writing.

The main body of evidence is the New Testament's use of the Old Testament. Matthew applies Hosea 11:1 to Jesus, although Hosea is plainly speaking of Israel's exodus. Paul in Galatians 3 places unusual weight on the singular form of "seed" in the Abrahamic promise. Hebrews reads Psalm 2, Psalm 8, Psalm 95, and Jeremiah 31 in ways that go beyond a narrowly contextual reading. The question is not whether these uses occur; the question is what hermeneutical account can explain them without making interpretation arbitrary.

This debate also shapes several other disputes. It affects how prophecy and fulfillment are understood, how typology functions, how the church relates to Israel, and whether the New Testament is clarifying the Old Testament or reframing it. In your repo, this file sits underneath the debates on dispensationalism and covenant theology, and it also connects directly to the Judaism vs. Christianity file on messianic prophecy.

## Key Definitions

### Literal-Grammatical-Historical Interpretation

**Literal-grammatical-historical interpretation** holds that the meaning of a text is fixed by the human author's intended meaning, as recoverable from the words, syntax, literary form, and historical setting. A text may have many applications, but it has one meaning. Later readers may draw implications, analogies, and theological consequences from that meaning, but they do not have the right to create a second meaning that was absent from the text's original communicative act.

### Canonical-Theological Interpretation

**Canonical-theological interpretation** holds that Scripture must be read as a unified whole because God is its ultimate author. This means a text can carry significance that is not exhausted by the conscious horizon of the human author. Later biblical revelation may disclose a fuller sense, trace typological patterns, or show that an earlier event, person, or institution pointed beyond itself in ways not immediately obvious from the original context alone.

### Single Meaning and Fuller Sense

**The single-meaning view** insists that a text has one meaning and that this meaning is determined by authorial intention in its historical setting. Any claim that a later interpreter has uncovered a new meaning is judged to be either application or distortion.

**The fuller-sense view** does not necessarily deny that texts have determinate meaning in their own context, but it argues that divine authorship allows Scripture to bear significance beyond what the human author fully understood. On this account, the New Testament may reveal dimensions of earlier texts that were really there by divine intention even if they were not transparent at the level of immediate historical reference.

### Typology

**In a more restrictive framework**, typology refers to real historical correspondences that are textually grounded and not simply imaginative parallels. The interpreter must show that the earlier pattern is actually present in the text and not imposed from outside.

**In a canonical framework**, typology is a structural feature of redemptive history. Persons, institutions, and events recur in escalated form across the canon. Adam, Israel, David, the temple, the exodus, and the sacrificial system are not merely isolated episodes. They are patterns that culminate in Christ and the new covenant order.

---

## Foundational Assumptions

### Position A assumes

The literal-grammatical-historical approach assumes that stable meaning requires a stable anchor. That anchor is the communicative intent of the text in its original setting. If meaning can expand beyond that horizon without strict controls, interpretation loses objectivity. This view also assumes that inerrancy is best protected when meaning is singular and historically grounded, since multiple layers of meaning can become a cover for readings that the text itself cannot reasonably bear.

### Position B assumes

The canonical-theological approach assumes that the Bible is not merely a library of religious documents but a providentially ordered canon. Because of that, the intention of the divine author cannot be reduced without remainder to the conscious horizon of the human author. This view also assumes that the New Testament's use of the Old Testament is not an embarrassment to be explained away, but an authorized model that Christian interpretation must learn to understand.

# Position A: Literal-Grammatical-Historical Interpretation: Strongest Case

---

## Argument 1: Meaning Is Fixed by the Original Context and Grammar

Every text is written by someone, to someone, in a language, within a historical situation. Meaning is therefore tied to that communicative event. Grammar, syntax, genre, and historical context are not optional background material. They are what make the text mean something definite rather than anything a later reader wants it to mean. If interpretation is detached from those controls, the result is no longer exegesis but theological appropriation.

> **P1:** The only objectively recoverable meaning of a text is the meaning intended in its original communicative setting.
>
> **P2:** Grammar, genre, and historical context are the proper means by which that meaning is recovered.
>
> **P3:** If later readers can generate new meanings beyond those controls, interpretation loses its objective boundary.
>
> **C:** The original context and grammar must control legitimate interpretation.

**Critique 1:** The New Testament itself often appears to read earlier texts in ways that are not limited to original historical meaning. Matthew 2:15 is the classic case: Hosea 11:1 refers to Israel's past exodus, yet Matthew applies it to Jesus.

**Critique 2:** The insistence on a single recoverable meaning can reflect modern assumptions about authorial intent more than ancient interpretive practice. Jewish interpreters in the Second Temple period were often more comfortable with layered readings.

**Rebuttal 1:** A distinction can be made between meaning and application. Matthew may be drawing a typological correspondence without claiming that Hosea originally predicted Jesus in a direct sense.

**Rebuttal 2:** Ancient plurality of readings does not itself establish legitimacy. The existence of multiple interpretations in history does not remove the need for criteria.

---

## Argument 2: Single Meaning Better Protects Interpretive Discipline

The strength of this approach is not only that it anchors meaning historically, but that it places a limit on what can be claimed in the name of Scripture. If a text can mean what the original author meant and also mean something quite different because of later canonical development, then it becomes difficult to say when an interpretation has gone too far. A disciplined method needs a stopping point. The literal-grammatical-historical approach provides one.

> **P1:** Responsible interpretation requires a principled way to distinguish valid readings from invalid ones.
>
> **P2:** Original-context meaning supplies a stable limit on interpretive claims.
>
> **P3:** Methods that allow later texts to disclose additional meaning risk blurring the line between interpretation and creative theological construction.
>
> **C:** A single-meaning method better preserves interpretive discipline.

**Critique 1:** This argument only works if the New Testament's own readings can be contained within the category of application rather than expanded meaning. In several cases that containment looks forced.

**Critique 2:** A strong concern for discipline can become a strong resistance to genuine textual development. The canon itself may exhibit an unfolding logic that this method is too rigid to capture.

**Rebuttal 1:** The burden of proof remains on the broader-reading view. It must explain how fuller sense avoids becoming a license for overreading.

**Rebuttal 2:** The fact that some NT uses are difficult does not justify weakening the controls on all interpretation.

---

## Argument 3: This Method Provides a Cleaner Basis for Prophecy and Israel

One reason this method has enduring appeal is that it handles promises, prophecies, and covenants in a direct way. If God promised land to Abraham's descendants, or restoration to Israel, those promises retain their ordinary reference unless the text itself redirects them. This gives the interpreter a clear basis for distinguishing Israel from the church and for resisting theological systems that absorb Old Testament promises into a broader and less textually explicit fulfillment scheme.

> **P1:** Covenants and prophecies are ordinarily understood according to their original referents unless the text itself clearly indicates otherwise.
>
> **P2:** Many Old Testament promises are addressed specifically to Israel in national and territorial terms.
>
> **P3:** Reassigning those promises to a broader canonical fulfillment often depends on later theological synthesis more than on the original wording.
>
> **C:** The literal-grammatical-historical approach offers a clearer and more textually stable reading of prophecy and Israel.

**Critique 1:** This strength is also one of the method's liabilities. It can produce a bifurcated Bible in which the Old Testament says one thing, the New Testament says another, and the unity of Scripture becomes harder to articulate.

**Critique 2:** The New Testament does at times appear to widen, deepen, or transform earlier categories, especially with respect to temple, seed, priesthood, and people of God.

**Rebuttal 1:** Apparent widening is not the same thing as replacement. A category can expand without losing its original referent.

**Rebuttal 2:** Theological unity should not be purchased at the cost of textual specificity.

---

## Pressure Points for LGH Interpretation

### Argument 1: The New Testament's Use of the Old Testament Presses Hard Against the Method

The most serious pressure point is simple: the New Testament does not always read the Old Testament the way this method says texts must be read. Matthew 2:15 is not an isolated curiosity. Galatians 3:16, Hebrews 4, Acts 2, and 1 Peter 2 all present similar tensions. These are not fringe examples. They are central to apostolic argumentation. If the apostolic writers repeatedly move beyond narrow historical reference, then a Christian hermeneutic that forbids such movement must explain why the apostles are allowed to do what the method itself rejects.

> **P1:** The New Testament regularly interprets Old Testament texts in ways that exceed narrow original-context reference.
>
> **P2:** The literal-grammatical-historical method says texts must be controlled by original-context meaning.
>
> **P3:** A Christian method must account for apostolic practice rather than treating it as a problem to be minimized.
>
> **C:** The method faces a major difficulty in explaining the New Testament's own hermeneutic.

**Critique 1:** The apostolic writers may be uniquely authorized to draw implications and typological correspondences because they write under inspiration. Their authority does not automatically establish an ordinary rule for later interpreters.

**Critique 2:** Some New Testament uses may be less about discovering hidden meanings and more about seeing Jesus as the climactic embodiment of existing patterns already present in the text.

**Rebuttal 1:** The appeal to unique apostolic authority solves part of the problem but not all of it. It explains why the apostles may speak this way. It does not explain what kind of interpretation they are actually doing.

**Rebuttal 2:** If the apostolic writers are tracing real textual patterns, then the method may need to be broadened so that those patterns can be recognized rather than treated as awkward exceptions.

### Argument 2: A Strict Single-Meaning Theory Can Be Too Narrow for a Theological Canon

The Bible is not only a set of local communicative acts. It is also a canon shaped over time. A method that treats every text as though its entire meaning is exhausted by the first moment of utterance can struggle to explain how later Scripture recontextualizes earlier Scripture. The issue is not whether context matters. It plainly does. The issue is whether immediate historical context is the only context that matters.

> **P1:** Biblical books do not remain isolated. They are placed within a developing canon that reuses, reframes, and connects earlier texts.
>
> **P2:** A strict single-meaning theory can leave little room for those canonical developments to count as interpretive significance rather than mere application.
>
> **P3:** Christian theology must explain the Bible as canon, not only as a sequence of local historical acts.
>
> **C:** The method can be too narrow if it treats original setting as the only relevant context.

**Critique 1:** Canonical placement does not erase original meaning. The first task of interpretation remains exegesis in the historical setting.

**Critique 2:** Once canonical placement is allowed to generate new meaning, interpreters need a firm rule to prevent theological improvisation.

**Rebuttal 1:** Those cautions are valid, but they show the need for constraint, not the impossibility of fuller significance.

**Rebuttal 2:** The stronger objection remains: if original context is treated as exhaustive, the canon's own internal development becomes harder to describe.

# Position B: Canonical-Theological Interpretation: Strongest Case

---

## Argument 1: The Bible Is a Unified Canon, Not a Loose Collection of Texts

The canonical approach begins from a theological claim about Scripture itself. The Bible is not merely a shelf of historically conditioned documents. It is a providentially ordered canon that tells one redemptive story. That claim does not cancel grammar or history. It situates them within a broader framework. Earlier texts still mean what they mean in their own setting, but they also participate in larger patterns that only become fully visible as revelation unfolds.

> **P1:** Scripture is a unified canon under divine authorship.
>
> **P2:** Texts within a canon can participate in patterns and trajectories that exceed the immediate awareness of their human authors.
>
> **P3:** Later revelation may therefore clarify the fuller significance of earlier revelation without contradicting it.
>
> **C:** Biblical interpretation must attend to canonical unity as well as original historical context.

**Critique 1:** This approach can sound attractive precisely because it is elastic. A broad appeal to divine authorship can be used to justify almost any theological synthesis if sufficient controls are not supplied.

**Critique 2:** The claim that Scripture is a unified story is itself an interpretive judgment. It is not a neutral datum that eliminates disagreement.

**Rebuttal 1:** The existence of abuse does not invalidate the principle. Every method can be abused, including historically focused ones that flatten theological continuity.

**Rebuttal 2:** The New Testament itself pushes readers toward canonical unity. Christian interpretation is not free to ignore that pressure.

---

## Argument 2: The New Testament Models a Canonical Reading of the Old Testament

The most compelling argument for this approach is not abstract but textual. The New Testament reads the Old Testament canonically. It sees Israel's story reaching a climax in Jesus. It reads the exodus, the temple, the kingdom, the sonship theme, and the covenant promises as converging in him. Some of these readings can be described as typological, some as eschatological intensification, some as direct fulfillment, but together they show that the apostolic writers did not restrict themselves to a narrowly atomized historical method.

> **P1:** The New Testament repeatedly interprets the Old Testament through patterns of fulfillment, recapitulation, and typology.
>
> **P2:** These interpretations are not occasional anomalies but central features of apostolic proclamation.
>
> **P3:** A Christian hermeneutic must explain and receive apostolic practice rather than treating it as a tolerated exception.
>
> **C:** The New Testament itself supports a canonical-theological approach.

**Critique 1:** Apostolic authority does not automatically tell later interpreters how far they may extend the same method. What the apostles can do under inspiration is not always a template for ordinary exegesis.

**Critique 2:** Some canonical readings may be better described as theological application after the meaning has been established historically, rather than as evidence for multiple layers of meaning in the earlier text.

**Rebuttal 1:** Even if apostolic authority is unique, the kind of reading the apostles perform still has to be described. A method that cannot describe it without reducing it to mere application is missing something important.

**Rebuttal 2:** In many cases the NT authors are not simply applying earlier texts. They are arguing from them as though those texts bear christological significance within the canon.

---

## Argument 3: Typology Fits the Shape of Redemptive History

Typology is not an arbitrary game of parallels. It arises from the repeated patterns built into the Bible's own story. Adam, Israel, David, priesthood, temple, sacrifice, exile, and exodus all reappear in forms that are both continuous and escalated. The canonical-theological approach argues that these repetitions are not accidental. They are part of the way Scripture teaches. Typology therefore belongs to exegesis when it is responsibly constrained by textual pattern, covenantal structure, and canonical development.

> **P1:** Scripture displays recurring historical and theological patterns across persons, institutions, and events.
>
> **P2:** The New Testament regularly identifies Christ as the climactic fulfillment of those patterns.
>
> **P3:** A method that excludes typological development will fail to capture an important feature of how Scripture relates its parts to one another.
>
> **C:** Responsible typology is necessary for a full reading of the canon.

**Critique 1:** The key word is "responsible." Typology can quickly become a way of baptizing imaginative parallels that the text itself does not support.

**Critique 2:** The more the method appeals to large patterns, the easier it becomes to minimize the local details of specific passages.

**Rebuttal 1:** Those dangers are real, which is why canonical reading still needs grammatical and historical discipline. The stronger forms of canonical interpretation do not abolish exegesis; they build on it.

**Rebuttal 2:** Local detail and canonical development do not have to compete. A text can retain its immediate sense while also participating in a larger pattern.

---

## Pressure Points for Canonical Interpretation

### Argument 1: It Can Drift into Uncontrolled Theological Overreading

The main risk of the canonical approach is that it can become difficult to say when an interpretation has exceeded the text. If the interpreter regularly appeals to divine authorship, redemptive patterns, and later revelation, there is always a temptation to import more into a passage than the passage can reasonably support. At that point typology collapses into pious allegory. The method needs constraints that are sharper than "the canon says so."

> **P1:** Canonical interpretation permits claims about significance that are not exhausted by original historical reference.
>
> **P2:** Without clear limits, this permission can become a license for overreading.
>
> **P3:** A method that cannot distinguish disciplined typology from imaginative allegory becomes unstable.
>
> **C:** Canonical interpretation faces a serious risk of overextension.

**Critique 1:** This risk is not unique to canonical approaches. Historically narrow readings can also become distortive by refusing to see textual connections the canon itself presents.

**Critique 2:** The best canonical interpreters do provide constraints: intertextual repetition, covenantal structure, explicit New Testament usage, and recurring theological patterns.

**Rebuttal 1:** Those constraints help, but they vary in clarity from case to case. Some proposed typologies are far better grounded than others.

**Rebuttal 2:** The pressure point remains. Canonical interpretation is strongest when it stays close to patterns already modeled in Scripture, and weakest when it generalizes beyond them.

### Argument 2: The Appeal to a Unified Canon Does Not End Disagreement

Saying that Scripture is one story does not settle what that story is. Different traditions tell the story differently. Covenant theology, progressive covenantalism, and dispensationalism all claim to honor the canon while arranging its major themes in different ways. The canonical approach therefore does not remove the need for argument. It relocates the argument to the level of narrative shape, covenant structure, and theological center.

> **P1:** Multiple theological systems claim to read Scripture canonically.
>
> **P2:** Canonical language by itself does not determine how Israel, church, kingdom, temple, and covenant should be related.
>
> **P3:** The method therefore requires further substantive judgments about the structure of biblical theology.
>
> **C:** Canonical interpretation does not eliminate disagreement; it shifts where the disagreement occurs.

**Critique 1:** That is true, but it is not a defect unique to the method. All hermeneutical frameworks require substantive judgments somewhere.

**Critique 2:** A historically narrow approach also makes major theological judgments, even when it presents itself as methodologically modest.

**Rebuttal 1:** Agreed. The significance of this pressure point is not that canonical interpretation is uniquely weak, but that it should not be treated as a shortcut past real exegetical disputes.

**Rebuttal 2:** The better versions of the method admit this openly and argue for a particular canonical shape rather than pretending the shape is self-evident.

# The Actual Hinge Points

Two questions control most of the dispute.

First, is meaning exhausted by what the human author consciously intended in the original setting, or can later revelation disclose a fuller sense that remains consistent with the earlier text? That question sits underneath almost every dispute about typology, prophecy, and apostolic use of the Old Testament. If meaning is fixed in a narrow author-centered way, many New Testament readings will look like overreach. If meaning can unfold within a canon governed by divine authorship, those same readings can be understood as legitimate developments rather than violations of the text.

Second, what kind of thing is typology? One option treats it as a limited interpretive judgment that must be tightly controlled by the original context and by clearly identifiable textual patterns. The other treats it as a feature of redemptive history itself, where recurring persons, institutions, and events are designed to culminate in Christ. That difference explains why the same reader can affirm grammatical-historical exegesis in principle yet diverge sharply when reading Hosea 11, Psalm 2, or the Abraham narratives in light of the New Testament.
