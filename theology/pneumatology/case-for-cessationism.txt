I lean toward classical cessationism. I believe the miraculous sign gifts described in the New Testament served a particular purpose during the establishment of the early church: confirming God's revelation and authenticating the apostolic proclamation of Christ.

Heb 1:1-2 tells us that God spoke formerly through the prophets, but has now spoken to us through His Son. Christ is the culmination of God's revelation. Heb 2:3-4 goes on to describe how the gospel was confirmed through signs, wonders, miracles, and gifts of the Holy Spirit. Paul makes a similar connection in 2 Cor 12:12, identifying signs and wonders as credentials of his apostleship. Eph 2:20 also describes the apostles and prophets as the foundation of the church, with Christ as its cornerstone. I believe these passages help explain the particular role miraculous gifts played during the apostolic era.

I realize there is no verse explicitly stating that the gifts would cease with the death of the apostles. I also understand the arguments from Acts 2, 1 Cor 12-14, and Eph 4. Those passages establish the Spirit's work and the importance of spiritual gifts in the church. The question is whether they establish that every miraculous gift must continue throughout the entire church age. I don't believe they do.

The argument from 1 Cor 13:8-12 deserves particular attention. I agree that Paul's description of seeing face to face and knowing fully points toward Christ's return. I don't think cessationism needs to rest on the claim that "the perfect" means the completed Bible. Even if Paul is referring to Christ's return, that passage alone doesn't settle when particular gifts ceased or whether all of them must continue until then.

I also want to clear up a common misunderstanding. Cessationism doesn't mean God stopped performing miracles or answering prayers. I believe God still heals and intervenes supernaturally. There is a difference between God performing a miracle and an individual possessing the spiritual gift of healing described in the New Testament.

I have similar questions about modern claims of prophecy and tongues. If prophecy is a message from God, on what biblical basis can it contain mistakes? And do modern claims of tongues actually correspond to what we see in Acts and 1 Cor 14? Those are reasonable questions, especially when personal experiences that cannot be independently verified are being used to establish dogmatic doctrine.

I don't question that people can have sincere and extraordinary experiences. I question whether those experiences demonstrate that the particular gifts described in the New Testament continue today.

I would also be dishonest if I left out how much my own observations have reinforced these convictions. I've witnessed far too many so called healing services that very obviously staged or fraudulent, displays of supposedly miraculous tongues that amounted to incoherent babbling, and people presenting ordinary statements or personal opinions as direct messages from God. I've also seen and personally experienced honest, cautious skepticism treated as satanic rebellion against God Himself, as though questioning someone's claim to divine revelation were itself sinful.

That last part particularly concerns me. When someone claims to have received a personal message directly from God, what meaningful standard can be used to challenge it? If the message is private, unverifiable, and presented as divine revelation, disagreement can easily be dismissed as a lack of faith or even opposition to God. When a supposed word from God contradicts Scripture, the person raising the objection is called a "Pharisee" or accused of "putting God in a box." If questioning a claim is treated as disobedience to God, the person making that claim is effectively shielded from accountability. That creates an environment in which manipulation and spiritual abuse can flourish. In some communities, someone claiming to be a faith healer, speak in tongues, or prophesy is revered when those same claims would warrant serious biblical scrutiny and possibly rebuke. Claims of divine revelation carry enormous authority, and those making them should be held accountable to Scripture without critics being accused of "touching God's anointed."

These experiences aren't my entire argument for cessationism, and I recognize that abuses do not prove genuine gifts cannot exist. They have, however, reinforced my concern about modern charismatic claims, particularly when the practices being defended bear so little resemblance to the purposes and descriptions of the gifts in Scripture.

I believe the apostles laid the foundation of the church, the gospel has been delivered, and the church continues under the authority of that apostolic witness. The Holy Spirit remains fully active in the church. I simply don't believe the miraculous sign gifts associated with that foundational period continue in the same way they did in the apostolic era.
