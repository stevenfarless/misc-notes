## Domain & Evidence

Both positions affirm that God is the omnipotent creator of all things, that he is perfectly good and perfectly loving, that history moves toward a determined eschatological endpoint, and that Scripture rather than philosophical speculation should govern conclusions about the divine nature.

Both employ literal-grammatical-historical hermeneutics in principle. Classical theists read the anthropomorphic and anthropopathic language of Scripture (God repenting, changing his mind, being grieved) as accommodation to human understanding, with the philosophical attributes of aseity, simplicity, immutability, and impassibility providing the interpretive framework. Open theists read the same anthropomorphic language as genuine disclosure of God's inner life and his real responsiveness to creatures, treating the accommodation framework as a Greek philosophical import that overrides what the texts plainly say.

This document maps each side's strongest case, their critiques of the opposing position, and the live hermeneutical and philosophical disputes, without forcing resolution.

The debate connects directly to the Calvinism vs. Arminianism document in this repo. Classical theism provides the philosophical infrastructure for Calvinist unconditional election: if God's decree, foreknowledge, and knowledge are coextensive, then his election of individuals to salvation is not conditioned on foreseen faith but on the eternal decree itself. Open theism sits at the far end of the free-will spectrum from Calvinism, cutting the foreknowledge-decree connection entirely. Arminianism occupies the middle ground: it affirms exhaustive divine foreknowledge while denying that foreknowledge is grounded in decree rather than in God's eternal prescience of free choices.

The live disagreements are specific: whether biblical prophecy requires exhaustive foreknowledge of all future free acts or only foreknowledge of what God himself intends to do, how to interpret the divine repentance texts, whether impassibility and immutability are biblical attributes or Hellenistic imports, whether genuine creaturely freedom is compatible with exhaustive divine foreknowledge, and whether the Molinist alternative successfully resolves the compatibility problem.

## Key Definitions

### Classical Theism

**Position A, classical theism:** God is eternal (existing outside temporal sequence or omnitemporally present to all moments), omniscient in the strong sense of knowing all true propositions including the complete future, immutable (his nature and knowledge admit of no change), and impassible (he is not acted upon by creatures in a way that produces new states in him). His foreknowledge of every event is exhaustive and certain, grounded in his own eternal nature and decree rather than in temporal observation. This is the position of the patristic tradition, scholastic theology, Augustine, Aquinas, the Reformed confessions, and the Westminster Standards.

**Position B, open theism:** classical theism has absorbed Hellenistic philosophical categories, particularly from Middle Platonism and Neoplatonism, that are foreign to the biblical portrait of God, and has systematically reinterpreted the biblical texts through that grid rather than allowing the texts to determine the doctrine.

### Open Theism

**Position B, open theism:** God is omniscient in the sense of knowing all knowable truths with perfect accuracy, but future free choices are not yet determinate and therefore not knowable even by God without compromising the genuine freedom of those choices. God knows every possibility and every probability exhaustively, foreknows everything he has determined to do, and responds to his creatures in genuine relationship. God is genuinely affected by what happens in the world, genuinely grieves over sin and suffering, and works within history toward his purposes by providence, persuasion, and response. Clark Pinnock, Gregory Boyd, John Sanders, and Richard Rice are the major contemporary proponents.

**Position A, classical theism:** open theism limits God's knowledge of the future in a way that undermines confidence in biblical prophecy, makes God's plans contingent on creaturely choices in ways that compromise his sovereignty, and has been recognized as outside the bounds of Christian orthodoxy by the Evangelical Theological Society and other bodies.

### Molinism (Mediating Position)

**Mediating position:** God possesses middle knowledge, knowledge of what every possible free creature would freely do in every possible circumstance (counterfactuals of creaturely freedom). This knowledge is logically prior to his creative decree. He actualizes the world whose sequence achieves his purposes without determining any free choice by direct decree. Exhaustive foreknowledge and libertarian freedom are both preserved through a distinct mechanism: not classical simple foreknowledge (knowing what will happen), not open theism's epistemic openness, but knowledge of what free creatures would do in every possible situation. Alvin Plantinga and William Lane Craig are the most prominent contemporary Molinists.

**Classical theism critique:** the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom have no truthmaker. There is no fact of the matter about what a free agent would do in a merely possible, non-actual situation; counterfactuals of freedom are not grounded in any state of affairs that could make them true, so middle knowledge is knowledge of propositions that are not true and cannot be true. This is the grounding objection.

**Open theism critique:** if God knows what you would freely do in every possible circumstance and then places you in the circumstance whose outcome serves his purposes, your freedom is functional but not genuine. The outcome is determined by the world God chose to actualize; you are free in the thin sense that no one coerced you, but God's selection of the world entailed your choice before you made it. The result is functionally equivalent to determinism even though the mechanism is not decree.

### Impassibility

**Position A:** God does not have emotions in the sense of being acted upon by creatures and moved into new affective states. He is the pure act of being, lacking all potentiality. The biblical language of divine grief, anger, love, and repentance is accommodation to human understanding, communicating real relational truths about God's disposition without implying that God undergoes emotional changes.

**Position B:** impassibility is a Neoplatonic attribute imported into Christian theology; the biblical God genuinely loves, grieves, responds, and is moved by his creatures, and the accommodation framework selectively suppresses what the texts say plainly in order to protect a philosophical concept that Scripture nowhere teaches.

### Divine Repentance

**Position A:** texts like Genesis 6:6 (God repented of making man), Exodus 32:14 (God repented of the evil he thought to do), and 1 Samuel 15:11 (God repented making Saul king) are anthropopathic, communicating God's genuine relational response in human terms. Numbers 23:19 (God is not a man that he should repent) and 1 Samuel 15:29 (the Strength of Israel does not lie or repent) provide the interpretive frame: the repentance language describes the creature-facing relational reality, not a change in God's eternal being.

**Position B:** the plain-sense reading of the repentance texts is that God genuinely changes his mind or course of action in response to changed circumstances or creaturely choices. Numbers 23:19 and 1 Samuel 15:29 are specific contextual statements about God not reneging on a particular promise or decision, not universal metaphysical claims about divine immutability overriding all other texts.

## Foundational Assumptions

### Position A assumes

Classical theists begin with **the aseity and simplicity of God** as the interpretive frame. God is the uncaused cause of all things, fully actual, owing his existence to nothing outside himself. A God who is acted upon by creatures, who undergoes new emotional states, or whose knowledge changes as the future unfolds is a God whose being depends partly on the world, which contradicts aseity. The philosophical attributes are not Greek imports but the necessary conceptual entailments of the biblical teaching that God is the creator of all things who owes his existence to nothing.

A further assumption is that **the prophetic texts require exhaustive foreknowledge**. Isaiah 46:9-10 (declaring the end from the beginning), Isaiah 44:7-8 (who predicted this long ago), and the detailed specificity of messianic prophecy across centuries all indicate a God who knows the future comprehensively, not merely the outcomes of his own intended acts.

### Position B assumes

Open theists begin with **the relational and responsive character of the biblical God** as the controlling frame. The biblical narrative presents God as genuinely engaging with creatures, responding to prayer, repenting, grieving, testing, asking questions, and discovering things in ways that the accommodation framework systematically suppresses. If the plain-sense portrayal of God in the narrative texts is allowed to set the agenda, a God of genuine relationship rather than impassible omniscience emerges.

A further assumption is that **genuine libertarian freedom requires epistemic openness**. If God knows with certainty what every creature will freely do before they do it, the question of how that foreknowledge is grounded becomes acute. Classical theism grounds it in the decree; Arminianism grounds it in simple foreknowledge of the future. Open theists argue that neither account preserves genuine freedom without implying that the future choices are already fixed in some sense, which makes freedom illusory.

# Position A: Classical Theism: Strongest Case

---

## Argument 1: The Prophetic Argument -- God Declares the End from the Beginning

Isaiah 46:9-10 presents God's ability to declare the end from the beginning as the distinguishing mark of the true God over against idols: "I am God, and there is no other; I am God, and there is none like me, declaring the end from the beginning and from ancient times things not yet done, saying, 'My counsel shall stand, and I will accomplish all my purpose.'" The argument is that the false gods of the nations cannot do this; Israel's God can. His ability to do so distinguishes him from the impotent idol, and the specificity of fulfilled prophecy demonstrates it historically. If God declares the end from the beginning across the whole of history, his foreknowledge extends to the complete future, including the free choices of creatures.

> **P1:** Isaiah 46:9-10 identifies God's ability to declare "from ancient times things not yet done" as the distinguishing mark of the true God.
>
> **P2:** The context contrasts God with idols that cannot predict, implying God's predictive capacity covers the kind of events idols would be expected to address: the full range of historical outcomes.
>
> **P3:** "Declaring the end from the beginning" across world history requires foreknowledge of the outcomes of free creaturely choices, since history is constituted by such choices.
>
> **C:** Isaiah 46 grounds God's unique identity in exhaustive foreknowledge of future history, which cannot be reduced to foreknowledge only of his own intended acts.

## Argument 2: Specific Fulfilled Prophecy Requires Foreknowledge of Free Acts

The Old Testament contains prophecies whose fulfillment required specific free choices by specific individuals. Isaiah 44:28-45:1 names Cyrus as the one who will authorize the rebuilding of Jerusalem and the temple, written at least 150 years before Cyrus was born. Micah 5:2 names Bethlehem as the birthplace of the coming ruler of Israel. Psalm 22 describes details of the crucifixion that require specific free choices by Roman soldiers, Jewish leaders, and bystanders. If God foreknew only what he himself intended to do, these prophecies could include only his own acts; the specific choices of named individuals who were not yet born could not be foreknown on open theism's account.

> **P1:** Isaiah 44:28-45:1 names Cyrus by name as the Persian king who would authorize Jerusalem's rebuilding, written more than a century before Cyrus existed.
>
> **P2:** Psalm 22 describes events at the crucifixion in detail, including the actions of individuals who would make free choices about how to treat Jesus.
>
> **P3:** On open theism, God foreknows only his own intended acts and probabilistic projections; he cannot foreknow the specific free choices of individuals not yet born.
>
> **C:** The specificity of fulfilled Old Testament prophecy involving named individuals and their free choices requires a foreknowledge that extends beyond God's own intentions, which open theism cannot provide.

## Argument 3: Divine Immutability and the Reliability of God's Promises

The biblical ground for confidence in God's promises is his unchangeable nature. Hebrews 6:17-18 states that God confirmed his promise with an oath so that by two unchangeable things it is impossible for God to lie. Malachi 3:6 grounds God's covenant faithfulness in his self-identical nature: "I the LORD do not change; therefore you, O children of Jacob, are not consumed." James 1:17 describes God as the Father of lights "with whom there is no variation or shadow due to change." These texts present immutability not as a Greek philosophical attribute but as the ground of the creature's confidence in God. A God who genuinely changes in knowledge, emotional state, or response to creatures introduces variation that these texts explicitly exclude.

> **P1:** Hebrews 6:17-18 grounds the reliability of God's promises in his unchangeable nature and his impossibility of lying.
>
> **P2:** Malachi 3:6 and James 1:17 present God's immutability as the basis for his creatures' stability and confidence.
>
> **P3:** A God whose knowledge of the future changes as free choices are made, or whose affective states are altered by creaturely events, introduces the kind of variation these texts explicitly deny.
>
> **C:** Biblical immutability, grounded in the texts themselves rather than in Greek philosophy, requires that God's knowledge and nature do not change in response to creaturely acts.

## Argument 4: The Foreknowledge-Providence Problem for Open Theism

If God does not know the future free choices of creatures, his providential governance of history toward its appointed end requires either constant intervention to correct unanticipated outcomes or a diminished eschatological certainty. Open theists argue that God works through persuasion and response. But the eschatological certainties of the New Testament, including the certain return of Christ, the certain resurrection of the dead, the certain final judgment, are presented as guaranteed outcomes, not probable projections. If history is constituted by free choices that God cannot foreknow, God's guarantee of a specific endpoint requires either that he will override freedom when necessary (reintroducing a form of determinism) or that the guarantee is probabilistic rather than certain, which is not how the New Testament presents it.

> **P1:** The New Testament presents eschatological outcomes including Christ's return, the resurrection, and the final judgment as certain rather than probable.
>
> **P2:** If history is constituted by free choices God cannot foreknow, certainty of a specific historical endpoint requires either deterministic override of freedom or probabilistic projection.
>
> **P3:** Neither option matches the New Testament's unconditional eschatological guarantees.
>
> **C:** Open theism cannot account for the New Testament's certain eschatological promises without either reintroducing a form of determinism or reducing the guarantees to probabilities.

## Position A -- Critique of Position B

### Critique 1: The Repentance Texts Are Anthropopathic by the Bible's Own Testimony

Open theism treats the divine repentance texts as plain-sense disclosure of God's inner life. Classical theists point out that the Bible provides its own interpretive key. Numbers 23:19 states: "God is not man, that he should lie, or a son of man, that he should change his mind." 1 Samuel 15:29 states: "the Glory of Israel will not lie or have regret, for he is not a man, that he should have regret." These statements occur in the same canon as the repentance texts. The Bible itself signals that the repentance language is not straightforward literal description of God's inner ontology but communication in human terms of a relational reality. Open theism overrides this internal canonical signal.

> **P1:** Numbers 23:19 and 1 Samuel 15:29 explicitly state that God does not repent or change his mind in the way a man does.
>
> **P2:** These texts are in the same canon as the repentance texts and provide the interpretive context for reading them.
>
> **P3:** Open theism reads the repentance texts as plain-sense disclosure while treating the non-repentance texts as contextually limited, but the burden is on the open theist to justify that asymmetric treatment.
>
> **C:** The Bible's own internal witness signals that the repentance language is anthropopathic; the plain-sense reading open theism applies to those texts contradicts the plain-sense reading of Numbers 23:19 and 1 Samuel 15:29.

### Critique 2: Open Theism Creates an Unreliable God

If God genuinely does not know what free creatures will do, he can be surprised, disappointed, and have his purposes frustrated by creaturely choices in ways that are not merely apparent but real. Open theists embrace this as the cost of genuine relationship. Classical theists respond that a God who can be genuinely surprised and whose plans can be genuinely frustrated is not the God of the biblical narrative, in which God's purposes stand (Isaiah 46:10), no purpose of his can be thwarted (Job 42:2), and he works all things according to the counsel of his will (Ephesians 1:11). The open theist account makes God a responsive participant in history rather than its sovereign author, which conflicts with the biblical presentation of God's unilateral guarantee of outcomes.

> **P1:** Isaiah 46:10, Job 42:2, and Ephesians 1:11 present God as working all things according to his purposes with no possibility of genuine frustration.
>
> **P2:** Open theism's God can be genuinely surprised by free choices and have his plans genuinely frustrated, requiring improvisation and response rather than sovereign governance.
>
> **P3:** A God whose plans are subject to genuine frustration and surprise does not match the biblical portrait of God as the sovereign author of history.
>
> **C:** Open theism's account of divine responsiveness is incompatible with the biblical texts that present God's purposes as indefeasibly certain.

### Critique 3: Open Theism Concedes the Theologically Decisive Move to the Problem of Evil

Open theism's primary motivation is theodicy: if God does not foreknow free choices, he is not responsible for the evil that results from them. Classical theists argue that this move concedes more than it gains. A God who cannot foreknow or prevent evil is not obviously more morally acceptable than a God who permits evil for good reasons; he may simply be a weaker God. Furthermore, the theodicy does not solve the problem of natural evil (earthquakes, disease, genetic disorders), which cannot be attributed to the free choices of human agents. And if God's lack of foreknowledge means he cannot guarantee the prevention of future catastrophic evil, the comfort the theodicy provides is purchased at the cost of God's reliability as protector and governor.

> **P1:** Open theism's primary theodicy move distances God from moral responsibility for evil by removing foreknowledge.
>
> **P2:** A God who lacks the power to foreknow and prevent evil is not a satisfying theodicy response; he is a weaker God rather than a morally cleaner one.
>
> **P3:** Natural evil, which is not attributable to human free choices, is not addressed by the open theist theodicy and remains a problem on the same terms as on classical theism.
>
> **C:** The open theist theodicy resolves one version of the problem of evil by introducing a God with reduced sovereignty, which creates its own set of problems rather than resolving theodicy.

## Position A -- Rebuttals to Position B

### Rebuttal 1: The Hellenistic Import Charge Revisited

Open theists argue that impassibility and immutability are Hellenistic imports. Classical theists respond that the genetic argument does not settle the exegetical case: the origin of a concept in Greek philosophy does not make it unbiblical if the biblical texts also teach it. The attributes of aseity and immutability are grounded in texts including Exodus 3:14 (God's self-identification as "I am who I am"), Psalm 102:27 (God remains the same while creation perishes), Malachi 3:6, and James 1:17. These texts were written before any influence of Platonic philosophy on Hebrew thought, and they teach the same attributes that the classical tradition later articulated with Platonic vocabulary. The vocabulary may be Greek; the content is biblical.

> **P1:** The classical attributes of immutability and aseity are taught by texts written prior to any Greek philosophical influence on Hebrew thought.
>
> **P2:** Exodus 3:14, Psalm 102:27, Malachi 3:6, and James 1:17 all teach that God does not change in a way that Greek philosophical categories merely articulated more precisely.
>
> **P3:** The genetic fallacy applies: origin in a Greek philosophical context does not make a doctrine unbiblical if the biblical texts teach it independently.
>
> **C:** The Hellenistic import charge is a genetic argument that does not address the exegetical case for immutability and impassibility; the texts must be engaged on their own terms.

### Rebuttal 2: Isaiah 46 Revisited

Open theists argue that Isaiah 46:10's declaration of the end from the beginning refers only to God's own purposes and intended acts, not to his foreknowledge of free creaturely choices. Classical theists respond that this reading is too narrow. The passage presents God's predictive capacity as covering the whole of history, not a curated list of his own intended acts. If Isaiah 46 only proves God foreknows his own decisions, it says nothing more than any rational agent with firm intentions could say, which would not distinguish the God of Israel from a sufficiently determined human being, let alone from the idols the passage is contrasting him with.

> **P1:** The purpose of Isaiah 46:9-10 is to distinguish the God of Israel from powerless idols by his capacity to know and declare the future.
>
> **P2:** If the passage proves only that God foreknows his own intentions, it provides no evidence that distinguishes him from any rational agent with firm intentions.
>
> **P3:** The apologetic force of the passage depends on God's foreknowledge covering the full range of historical events, not only his own decisions.
>
> **C:** The open theist restriction of Isaiah 46 to divine intentions collapses the passage's apologetic argument and makes it less impressive than classical theism's reading requires.

### Rebuttal 3: Exhaustive Foreknowledge Does Not Entail Determinism

Open theists argue that exhaustive foreknowledge implies determinism: if God knows with certainty what you will do, it is necessary that you do it, which undermines freedom. Classical theists respond that this inference confuses epistemic necessity with causal necessity. God's knowing what you will freely do does not cause you to do it; it simply means that what you will freely do is known in advance. A time-traveler who watched you make a choice yesterday knows what you chose; that knowledge did not determine your choice. God's eternal knowledge of the future is analogous: his knowing does not cause; it registers what is the case, including what free creatures will cause by their own choices.

> **P1:** The inference from foreknowledge to determinism confuses epistemic necessity (if God knows X, X must occur) with causal necessity (God's knowing X causes X to occur).
>
> **P2:** Knowledge of a free act does not retroactively determine the act; it registers what the free agent will in fact choose.
>
> **P3:** Classical theism holds that God's knowledge of future free choices is not the causal basis of those choices but the eternal registration of them.
>
> **C:** Exhaustive foreknowledge is compatible with libertarian freedom if the foreknowledge is not the causal ground of the foreknown act, and classical theism holds precisely this.

### Rebuttal 4: The Testing and Discovery Texts Revisited

Open theists cite texts in which God tests creatures to find out what is in their hearts (Genesis 22:12, "now I know that you fear God"; Deuteronomy 8:2) as evidence that God genuinely lacks foreknowledge. Classical theists respond that these texts describe the testing event in terms of its human-facing purpose and pedagogical function rather than its epistemological function for God. The testing of Abraham was not God gathering information he lacked; it was the event through which Abraham's faith was demonstrated, confirmed, and made historically real. The "now I know" language describes the outcome of the test in relational and narrative terms, not God's epistemic state before and after.

> **P1:** Genesis 22:12's "now I know" is the climax of a testing narrative whose purpose is the demonstration and confirmation of Abraham's faith, not an account of God's epistemology.
>
> **P2:** The narrative function of a testing event is to make something historically real and relationally demonstrated; it does not require that God lacked the information prior to the test.
>
> **P3:** Deuteronomy 8:2 frames the wilderness testing in terms of its purpose for Israel, not as an account of God's knowledge acquisition.
>
> **C:** The testing texts describe the human-facing purpose and relational function of the events; they do not report God's epistemic limitations.

# Position B: Open Theism: Strongest Case

---

## Argument 1: The Biblical Portrait of a Responsive God

The biblical narrative consistently presents God as genuinely responding to creaturely events in ways that are not merely apparent but real. God grieves that he made humanity (Genesis 6:6). He repents of the harm he planned (Exodus 32:14; Jonah 3:10). He is moved by Israel's groaning (Exodus 2:24-25) and by Moses' intercession (Exodus 32:11-14). He tests Abraham to see what he will do (Genesis 22:1-12). He asks Adam "where are you?" (Genesis 3:9). He asks Cain "where is your brother?" (Genesis 4:9). The cumulative pattern across hundreds of narrative texts presents a God who is genuinely in relationship, genuinely affected, and genuinely responsive. If the accommodation framework suppresses this entire portrait to protect a philosophical attribute, the accommodation is doing more work than exegesis warrants.

> **P1:** Dozens of biblical texts present God as genuinely grieving, repenting, being moved, responding to prayer, testing to discover, and asking questions.
>
> **P2:** The accommodation framework treats all of these texts as non-literal in order to protect the philosophical attributes of impassibility and omniscience.
>
> **P3:** When an interpretive framework must non-literally read a large proportion of the primary texts to preserve a conclusion, the framework is overriding the texts rather than being derived from them.
>
> **C:** The biblical portrait of a genuinely responsive, grieving, and relationally engaged God is the plain-sense reading of a large body of narrative text; the accommodation framework is a system imposed on those texts from outside.

## Argument 2: Genuine Freedom Requires an Open Future

If God knows with exhaustive certainty what every creature will freely do in every situation before those creatures make any choices, the future is already fixed in some sense. Open theists argue that a fixed future is incompatible with genuine libertarian freedom: if it is already true that you will choose A tomorrow, then it is not genuinely possible for you to choose not-A. The choice is determined, even if not by God's decree directly. The only way to preserve genuine freedom in the libertarian sense, the ability to have done otherwise in the same circumstances, is to hold that future free choices are not yet determinate and therefore not yet knowable, even by God.

> **P1:** Libertarian freedom requires the ability to have done otherwise in the same circumstances, meaning both options must be genuinely possible at the time of choice.
>
> **P2:** If it is already eternally true that you will choose A, then choosing not-A was never genuinely possible, and your freedom is illusory even if no one coerced you.
>
> **P3:** Genuine freedom requires that future free choices not be already determined as true or false before they are made, which means they are not yet knowable.
>
> **C:** Exhaustive foreknowledge of future free acts is incompatible with libertarian freedom; an open future is the necessary condition for genuine creaturely agency.

## Argument 3: The Divine Repentance Texts Resist the Accommodation Framework

The accommodation framework treats the divine repentance texts as non-literal. But 1 Samuel 15 creates a direct internal tension that the framework cannot smoothly resolve: in verse 11 God says "I regret that I made Saul king," and in verse 29 Samuel says "the Glory of Israel will not lie or repent." Classical theists use verse 29 to read verse 11 non-literally. Open theists argue the reverse is also legitimate: the narrative function of verse 11 is to report God's genuine response to Saul's disobedience, and verse 29 is a specific contextual statement about God not reneging on the judgment he has just pronounced through Samuel, not a universal metaphysical principle overriding all other texts. The same tension appears in Numbers 23:19 alongside Jonah 3:10, where God does repent of the evil he planned against Nineveh. The Bible's own internal usage does not allow a simple rule in which non-repentance texts always interpret repentance texts.

> **P1:** 1 Samuel 15 contains both a divine repentance statement (v. 11) and a divine non-repentance statement (v. 29) in the same chapter, applied to the same situation.
>
> **P2:** Numbers 23:19 (God does not repent) and Jonah 3:10 (God repented of the evil he planned) both belong to the canon; neither can simply override the other as a universal interpretive rule.
>
> **P3:** The contextual reading of the non-repentance texts as specific situational statements is as exegetically defensible as reading them as universal metaphysical principles.
>
> **C:** The accommodation framework's use of non-repentance texts to interpret repentance texts is a hermeneutical choice, not an exegetically forced conclusion; the open theist reading of the repentance texts as genuine disclosure is equally defensible.

## Argument 4: Open Theism Provides a Coherent Theodicy That Classical Theism Cannot Match

If God has exhaustive foreknowledge of all future events including all evil, and if that foreknowledge is grounded in his decree (Calvinism) or in his eternal registration of what will occur (Arminianism), God is in some sense complicit in every evil that occurs. On Calvinism, God decreed the evil. On Arminianism, God knew with certainty that the evil would occur and created the world anyway. Open theism removes God's prior certain knowledge of specific evils: God created a world with genuinely free creatures knowing that evil was a risk, but not foreknowing specific evils that would result. The moral distinction between knowingly permitting a specific evil and creating a world in which evil is a known possibility parallels the distinction between a parent who watches a specific crime occur and one who raises children in a world where crime is possible.

> **P1:** Classical theism (whether Calvinist or Arminian) holds that God had certain foreknowledge of every specific evil that has occurred before he created the world.
>
> **P2:** The moral distinction between foreknowing a specific evil and permitting it, versus creating a world in which evil is a possibility without specific foreknowledge, is a genuine moral distinction.
>
> **P3:** Open theism preserves that distinction by removing specific foreknowledge of specific evils from God's pre-creative knowledge.
>
> **C:** Open theism provides a more coherent theodicy than classical theism by removing God's specific prior certain knowledge of particular evils while retaining his sovereign purpose and general foreknowledge of his own intentions.

## Position B -- Critique of Position A

### Critique 1: Classical Theism Is Built on Hellenistic Philosophy, Not Biblical Exegesis

The attributes of divine simplicity, impassibility, timelessness, and actus purus are not derived from exegesis of biblical texts; they are derived from Aristotelian and Neoplatonic theology and were systematized by patristic and scholastic theologians who were deeply formed by those traditions. Tertullian famously asked what Athens has to do with Jerusalem. The answer, for classical theism, is a great deal: the God of classical theism is recognizably the Unmoved Mover of Aristotle and the One of Plotinus before he is the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. The biblical God weeps, repents, responds, loves, and grieves; the God of Aristotle and Plotinus does none of these things. When the classical tradition reads the biblical texts through that grid, it is importing the philosophical conclusion and making the exegesis serve it.

> **P1:** The core attributes of classical theism, simplicity, actus purus, timelessness, and strict impassibility, are systematized forms of Aristotelian and Neoplatonic theology.
>
> **P2:** These attributes are not the conclusions of exegesis of biblical texts; they are philosophical framework commitments applied to the biblical texts.
>
> **P3:** The God who results from reading the Bible through that framework is more similar to the Aristotelian Unmoved Mover than to the God who weeps over Lazarus, repents over Saul, and grieves over Israel.
>
> **C:** Classical theism is a Hellenistic philosophical system imposed on the biblical texts rather than derived from them; the open theist alternative takes the biblical texts as they stand.

### Critique 2: The Accommodation Framework Is Unfalsifiable

Classical theism's accommodation framework functions as a closed interpretive system: every text that presents God as responsive, emotional, or changing is read as accommodation; every text that presents God as immutable or omniscient is read as literal. There is no text that could, in principle, falsify the attributes of impassibility or exhaustive foreknowledge, because any counterevidence is absorbed by the accommodation category. An interpretive framework that cannot be falsified by any text is not derived from the texts; it is imposed on them. Open theism operates with a simpler rule: read the texts as they stand unless the immediate context or canonical signal requires otherwise.

> **P1:** The accommodation framework reads every responsive, emotional, or changing God-text as non-literal, protecting the philosophical attributes from any textual challenge.
>
> **P2:** A framework that can absorb any counterevidence without revision is not falsifiable; an unfalsifiable interpretive framework is not derived from the texts.
>
> **P3:** Open theism's simpler rule, read the texts as they stand unless context requires otherwise, applies a standard hermeneutical principle without preemptively protecting a philosophical conclusion.
>
> **C:** Classical theism's accommodation framework is unfalsifiable by the texts it interprets; that is a methodological failure, not an exegetical virtue.

### Critique 3: Classical Theism Makes Prayer Incoherent

If God's knowledge and will are eternal, immutable, and complete from before creation, then petitionary prayer cannot change anything. What will happen is already determined and known from eternity; no prayer could alter the fixed divine knowledge or will. Classical theists typically respond that God's eternal decree includes the prayers as means to their appointed ends, so prayer is not futile. Open theists argue this is a verbal solution: if the outcome was already determined and the prayer was always going to occur as part of the decree, then the prayer was not genuinely causally efficacious in any meaningful sense. God was not moved by the prayer; he merely decreed that the prayer would precede the outcome. Open theism makes prayer genuinely efficacious because God did not know in advance whether the prayer would be offered, and his response to it is real.

> **P1:** If God's will and knowledge are eternal and immutable, petitionary prayer cannot introduce any new information or change any outcome that was not already determined from eternity.
>
> **P2:** The classical response that prayer is included in the decree as a means makes prayer a predetermined mechanism rather than a genuine communication that moves God.
>
> **P3:** Open theism makes prayer genuinely causally efficacious: God did not know in advance whether and how you would pray, and his response is real rather than pre-scripted.
>
> **C:** Classical theism makes petitionary prayer functionally incoherent; open theism preserves its genuine efficacy by making God's response to prayer a real rather than pre-determined event.

## Position B -- Rebuttals to Position A

### Rebuttal 1: The Prophetic Argument Revisited

Classical theists argue that specific fulfilled prophecy requires exhaustive foreknowledge of free creaturely choices. Open theists respond that most biblical prophecy is conditional (Jeremiah 18:7-10: if a nation repents, God will relent of judgment; if it does evil, he will bring judgment), and that even unconditional prophecy can be accounted for by God's sovereign determination to bring about specific events without requiring foreknowledge of every intervening free choice. God determined to raise up Cyrus and moved in history to bring it about; he did not need to foreknow every free choice Cyrus would make in order to decree and accomplish that outcome. The prophecy tracks God's own sovereign purposes, not his registration of free choices.

> **P1:** Most Old Testament prophecy is conditional: Jeremiah 18:7-10 explicitly frames prophecy in terms of divine response to human behavior, not fixed foreknowledge.
>
> **P2:** Unconditional prophecy can be grounded in God's sovereign determination to accomplish specific outcomes without requiring foreknowledge of every free choice involved.
>
> **P3:** Prophesying that Cyrus will issue a decree requires that God will ensure Cyrus does so; it does not require that God foreknew every free choice Cyrus would make independently of that determination.
>
> **C:** Biblical prophecy is accounted for by God's sovereign purposes and determinations; it does not require exhaustive foreknowledge of all future free creaturely choices.

### Rebuttal 2: The Foreknowledge-Freedom Incompatibility Revisited

Classical theists argue that foreknowledge does not cause foreknown acts and therefore does not compromise freedom. Open theists respond that the causal point misses the logical point: the issue is not whether God's knowledge causes the act but whether the act was already determined as true before it occurred. If it was already eternally true that you would choose A, then the proposition "you will choose A" was true before you existed, which means the choice was already fixed. Whether that fixity is caused by God's knowledge, by God's decree, or by an eternal truth about the future, the result is the same: when you make the choice, it was never genuinely possible for you to choose otherwise, because it was already true that you would not.

> **P1:** The issue is logical determination, not causal determination: if "you will choose A" is eternally true, then choosing not-A was never genuinely possible regardless of what made it true.
>
> **P2:** Classical theism's response that foreknowledge does not cause the act does not address the logical point that a fixed future truth forecloses genuine alternatives.
>
> **P3:** Whether the future is fixed by decree, by simple foreknowledge, or by eternal truth, the result is the same: genuine libertarian freedom requires that neither alternative is already determined as true or false.
>
> **C:** The foreknowledge-freedom incompatibility is a logical problem about the fixity of the future, not a causal problem; the classical response addresses only the causal version.

### Rebuttal 3: Open Theism Does Not Reduce God to a Finite Being

Classical theists argue that open theism makes God ignorant and therefore less than the God of Scripture. Open theists respond that God's knowledge is still incomprehensibly vast: he knows all of the past perfectly, all of the present perfectly, every possibility and probability exhaustively, every possible outcome of every possible free choice, and everything he himself has determined to do. The limitation is not on God's cognitive capacity but on the nature of the object of knowledge: future free choices are not yet determinate, so there is nothing to know. An omniscient God perfectly knows everything that is knowable; if future free choices are not yet facts, God's failure to know them is not a limitation on his omniscience but a recognition of what is not yet the case.

> **P1:** Open theism does not limit God's cognitive capacity; it limits the domain of knowable facts by holding that future free choices are not yet determinate.
>
> **P2:** If future free choices are not yet facts, then a perfectly omniscient God who knows all facts does not know them, not because of cognitive limitation but because there is nothing there to know.
>
> **P3:** God's knowledge of all past facts, all present facts, all possibilities, all probabilities, and all his own intentions remains exhaustive and perfect on open theism.
>
> **C:** Open theism's God is not ignorant in any pejorative sense; he knows everything that is the case, and the limitation is on what is the case rather than on his knowledge of it.

### Rebuttal 4: The Eschatological Certainty Problem Revisited

Classical theists argue that open theism cannot guarantee certain eschatological outcomes. Open theists respond that God's sovereign power to accomplish his purposes is not dependent on foreknowledge. God does not need to foreknow what creatures will freely do in order to guarantee that his purposes will prevail; he is powerful enough to work through, around, and in response to creaturely choices to bring history to its appointed end. The certainty of eschatological outcomes is grounded in God's power and determination, not in his foreknowledge. A general who knows the terrain, has superior resources, and is willing to adapt his tactics can guarantee the outcome of a battle without needing to foreknow every free choice his troops and opponents will make.

> **P1:** Eschatological certainty can be grounded in God's sovereign power and determination rather than in his foreknowledge of every free choice that will occur.
>
> **P2:** A sufficiently powerful agent can guarantee an outcome by working through and in response to events without needing to foreknow every free choice involved.
>
> **P3:** The New Testament's eschatological certainties are grounded in what God will do, not in his foreknowledge of what free creatures will do.
>
> **C:** Open theism can preserve the certainty of eschatological outcomes by grounding them in God's sovereign determination and power rather than in exhaustive foreknowledge.

---

# The Three Actual Hinges

The real issue is not whether God is omniscient or whether he is sovereign. Both positions affirm both. The real issue is **the scope of omniscience with respect to future free acts**: whether an omniscient God perfectly knows all that is knowable and future free acts are not yet in the domain of the knowable (open theism), or whether an omniscient God knows the complete future because his eternal knowledge is not limited by temporal sequence (classical theism). Both sides have a principled answer; neither is obviously forced by the texts, and both depend partly on prior philosophical commitments about the nature of time, freedom, and knowledge.

A second hinge is **the hermeneutical rule for the anthropomorphic and anthropopathic texts**. Classical theism applies the accommodation framework systematically; open theism applies plain-sense reading by default. Neither rule is itself derived from a single text; both are hermeneutical commitments brought to the texts. Classical theism's rule is internally consistent but functionally unfalsifiable by the narrative texts. Open theism's rule takes the narrative texts at face value but must explain how the non-repentance texts (Numbers 23:19; 1 Samuel 15:29) relate to the repentance texts without simply reversing the classical theist's move.

The third hinge is **whether Molinism dissolves the bilateral tension**. Molinism claims to preserve both exhaustive foreknowledge and genuine libertarian freedom through middle knowledge of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom. If the grounding objection succeeds, counterfactuals of creaturely freedom have no truthmaker and middle knowledge is knowledge of nothing, leaving the classical theist needing another mechanism and the open theist's critique unanswered. If the grounding objection fails and counterfactuals of freedom are coherent objects of knowledge, Molinism provides a genuine third option. Whether Molinism succeeds is partly a metaphysical question about the ontological grounds of modal truths, and it is the most technically demanding open question in this debate.
