# Part One: Debate Outline

---

## Domain & Evidence

This debate is about the boundaries of the Old Testament canon. The specific question is whether the seven deuterocanonical books (Tobit, Judith, 1-2 Maccabees, Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, Baruch) and several additions to Daniel and Esther carry the same authority as the protocanonical books.

The main body of evidence consists of four textual and historical data points. The Masoretic Text, which forms the basis for the Protestant Old Testament, excludes these books. The Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Old Testament used widely by the early church, includes them. The Dead Sea Scrolls contain fragments of some deuterocanonical texts, showing they circulated in pre-Christian Jewish communities. The Council of Trent (1546) formally defined the deuterocanon as canonical for the Catholic Church, partly in response to the Reformation.

This debate also has downstream consequences. Doctrines with thin support in the protocanon, including prayer for the dead (2 Maccabees 12) and certain formulations of merit and justification (Sirach), gain additional textual grounding if the deuterocanon is received as Scripture. For conditional immortality and annihilationism, the deuterocanon is largely a peripheral matter, but it is relevant where 2 Maccabees touches on resurrection and the state of the dead.

---

## Key Definitions

### Deuterocanon

**Deuterocanon** refers to the books included in the Septuagint but absent from the Masoretic Text. Catholic and Orthodox traditions receive them as fully canonical. Protestants call them the Apocrypha and treat them as useful for reading but not authoritative for doctrine. The term means "belonging to a second canon," coined to distinguish them from the protocanon without denying their value.

### Protocanon

**Protocanon** refers to the books whose canonical status was never disputed: the thirty-nine books shared by Protestant, Catholic, and Orthodox Old Testaments.

### Masoretic Text

**The Masoretic Text** is the standardized Hebrew text of the Old Testament, finalized by Jewish scribes (Masoretes) roughly between the 7th and 10th centuries CE. It does not include the deuterocanonical books and forms the text base for the Protestant Old Testament.

### Septuagint

**The Septuagint** is the Greek translation of the Old Testament, produced in stages from roughly the 3rd to 1st centuries BCE. It includes the deuterocanonical books and was the primary Old Testament text used by the early church and cited by New Testament authors.

### Apocrypha

**Apocrypha** is the Protestant label for the deuterocanonical books, derived from a Greek word meaning "hidden." The term carries an implicit judgment that these books, while historically interesting, are not Scripture.

---

## Foundational Assumptions

### Position A assumes

The inclusion position assumes that the Septuagint, as the Old Testament received and used by Christ, the apostles, and the early church, carries canonical authority. It also assumes that the Jewish community's later narrowing of their own canon in the late first century CE does not retroactively determine what Christians receive as Scripture. On this view, the Council of Trent did not add books to the Bible; it formally affirmed a canon that had been in continuous use.

### Position B assumes

The exclusion position assumes that the Jewish community's recognition of a book as Scripture before Christ is the decisive criterion for Old Testament canon boundaries. It also assumes that doctrinal novelty, books that introduce teachings absent from the protocanon, is a negative mark against canonicity. On this view, the Reformers did not remove books from the Bible; they restored the canon to its original Jewish boundaries.

---

# Position A: Include the Deuterocanon as Scripture: Strongest Case

---

## Argument 1: The Septuagint Was the Old Testament of the Early Church

The early church did not read the Hebrew Masoretic Text. It read the Septuagint. New Testament authors quote the Old Testament predominantly from the Septuagint, including in passages where the Septuagint and Masoretic Text differ. If the apostolic community received the Septuagint as its Scripture, and the Septuagint includes the deuterocanon, then the early church's operative Old Testament was the broader canon.

> **P1:** The New Testament authors quote the Old Testament primarily from the Septuagint.
>
> **P2:** The Septuagint includes the deuterocanonical books as part of its text.
>
> **P3:** The canon received and used by the apostolic community has prima facie authority for defining Christian Scripture.
>
> **C:** The deuterocanon belongs in the Christian Old Testament by the same logic that makes the Septuagint authoritative for the early church.

**Critique 1:** Quoting from the Septuagint translation does not mean the entire contents of Septuagint manuscripts were treated as canonical. Ancient manuscripts bundled books together without implying every included book had equal authority.

**Critique 2:** Some Septuagint manuscripts vary in their contents. There was no single fixed Septuagint canon analogous to the fixed Masoretic Text.

**Rebuttal 1:** The bundling objection is valid, but it cuts both ways. If we cannot infer canonical status from Septuagint inclusion, we also cannot infer non-canonical status from Masoretic exclusion alone.

**Rebuttal 2:** Variation in manuscripts complicates both positions, but the consistent pattern of early church usage across multiple texts and regions supports a broader canon rather than a narrower one.

---

## Argument 2: The Jewish Canon Was Not Fixed Before Christ

The claim that the Jewish community excluded the deuterocanon before Christ is historically contested. The Masoretic Text reflects a standardization process that was still underway in the first century CE. The Dead Sea Scrolls show that Second Temple Jewish communities valued and copied deuterocanonical texts. The Council of Jamnia, often cited as the moment when the Jewish canon was fixed, is itself disputed as a formal council with binding authority.

> **P1:** The Jewish canon was not definitively fixed as the twenty-four-book Hebrew canon before the first century CE.
>
> **P2:** Second Temple Jewish communities, including Qumran, used and preserved deuterocanonical texts.
>
> **P3:** The post-70 CE rabbinic narrowing of the Jewish canon may reflect polemical concerns after the destruction of the temple rather than a continuous tradition.
>
> **C:** The argument from pre-Christian Jewish canon boundaries is weaker than it appears, since those boundaries were not settled during the period in question.

**Critique 1:** The Dead Sea Scrolls also contain many non-canonical texts. Copying or preserving a book does not establish that the community treated it as Scripture.

**Critique 2:** Even if the Jewish canon was fluid before 70 CE, the Reformation's appeal to the Hebrew canon has a principled basis: it aligns Old Testament canon with the text Jesus and his contemporaries would have known in synagogue practice.

**Rebuttal 1:** The distinction between valued-but-not-canonical and canonical is real, but it requires evidence beyond mere presence in a collection. That evidence is disputed for the deuterocanon in both directions.

**Rebuttal 2:** Synagogue practice in the first century drew on a variety of texts. The narrower Hebrew canon reflects a later standardization, not an unbroken pre-Christian practice.

---

## Pressure Points for Inclusion

### Argument 1: Some Deuterocanonical Doctrines Sit Uneasily with the Protocanon

The inclusion position has to account for passages in the deuterocanon that appear to support doctrines either absent from or in tension with the protocanon. Second Maccabees 12:43-46 supports prayers and offerings for the dead as an aid to their resurrection. Sirach 3:30 and Tobit 4:10 link almsgiving with atonement for sin. If these books are fully canonical, they generate doctrinal pressure that Protestant interpreters have consistently resisted.

> **P1:** The deuterocanon contains passages that support prayers for the dead and merit-based atonement.
>
> **P2:** These doctrines have thin or no support in the protocanon.
>
> **P3:** A canon that introduces significant doctrinal novelty without clear grounding in the primary texts is suspect.
>
> **C:** Inclusion requires accounting for doctrinal additions that the protocanon does not clearly support.

**Critique 1:** Doctrinal novelty relative to Protestant readings of the protocanon is not the same as doctrinal novelty relative to the text itself. The Protestant canon is already an interpretive choice.

**Critique 2:** The deuterocanon also contains passages that align well with Protestant theology, including strong monotheism, detailed wisdom literature, and historical narrative with clear moral framing.

**Rebuttal 1:** The specific passages on prayer for the dead and meritorious almsgiving are not peripheral. They bear on central questions about the nature of salvation and the state of the dead, which makes the doctrinal-novelty concern harder to dismiss.

**Rebuttal 2:** Canonical coherence does not require every passage to have a parallel elsewhere, but it does require that new doctrinal claims not contradict the overall witness of Scripture.

### Argument 2: The Catholic Canon Pre-dates the Reformation

The inclusion position must still explain why the deuterocanon, if it was part of the church's Scripture from early on, was treated with varying degrees of authority by figures like Jerome, who preferred the Hebrew canon, and Augustine, who supported the broader canon. The historical record shows that the status of these books was debated within the church long before Trent formalized one position.

> **P1:** Early church figures disagreed about the canonical status of the deuterocanon.
>
> **P2:** Jerome, arguably the most influential early Bible scholar, preferred the Hebrew canon as the Old Testament standard.
>
> **P3:** Trent's definition was a formal response to the Reformation, not simply a restatement of undisputed tradition.
>
> **C:** The claim that the deuterocanon had settled canonical status before the Reformation overstates the historical consensus.

**Critique 1:** Jerome's preference was a minority position in the Western church. Augustine's broader canon was more widely followed and shaped the Old Latin and Vulgate tradition received by the medieval church.

**Critique 2:** The fact that a question was debated does not mean it was unresolved. Many settled doctrines were disputed before formal definition.

**Rebuttal 1:** Both points are fair, but the debate among the fathers shows that the deuterocanon's status was genuinely open rather than simply assumed. Trent resolved a real dispute rather than ratifying a seamless tradition.

# Position B: Exclude the Deuterocanon from the Canon: Strongest Case

---

## Argument 1: The Jewish Community Defined the Old Testament Canon Before Christ

Jesus and the apostles operated within Second Temple Judaism. Their canon was the Hebrew Scripture recognized in that community. The New Testament never quotes the deuterocanonical books as Scripture using the standard citation formulas (e.g., "it is written," "Scripture says"). The Reformers' appeal to the Hebrew canon was therefore not an innovation. It was a return to the text base that Jesus himself used.

> **P1:** Jesus and the apostles treated the Hebrew Scriptures as their authoritative Old Testament.
>
> **P2:** The New Testament does not cite deuterocanonical books using the citation formulas reserved for Scripture.
>
> **P3:** Aligning the Christian Old Testament with the Hebrew canon matches the practice of Christ and the apostles.
>
> **C:** The Protestant canon correctly identifies the Old Testament by following the text base received in the Jewish community Jesus inhabited.

**Critique 1:** The New Testament also does not formally cite every book in the Hebrew canon. Absence of citation is not the same as exclusion.

**Critique 2:** The New Testament does contain possible allusions to deuterocanonical texts, including Hebrews 11 and Wisdom of Solomon. The line between allusion and citation is not always clear.

**Rebuttal 1:** The absence-of-citation point is valid but limited. The consistent pattern is that direct citations with canonical formulas track closely to the Hebrew canon, while deuterocanonical material appears only as background or allusion.

**Rebuttal 2:** Allusion is a lower bar than citation. Drawing on a text for illustration or vocabulary does not make it Scripture.

---

## Argument 2: The Deuterocanon Introduces Doctrines Absent from the Protocanon

The most practically significant argument for exclusion is doctrinal. If the deuterocanon is Scripture, then prayer for the dead and meritorious almsgiving have canonical support. These doctrines are not merely absent from the protocanon; they are in tension with its overall witness on the nature of salvation and the finality of death. A canon that supports such doctrines requires Protestant interpreters to revise or complicate positions that are otherwise clearly grounded in the thirty-nine books.

> **P1:** The deuterocanon contains passages supporting prayers for the dead and meritorious almsgiving.
>
> **P2:** These doctrines are not clearly present in the protocanon and arguably contradict its overall witness.
>
> **P3:** Canonical books should not introduce doctrinal claims that contradict or significantly extend the core witness of Scripture without strong corroborating support.
>
> **C:** The doctrinal content of certain deuterocanonical passages is a mark against their canonical status.

**Critique 1:** Whether these passages contradict the protocanon depends on prior interpretive decisions about the protocanon itself. The argument is somewhat circular.

**Critique 2:** The protocanon itself contains passages that require careful handling to avoid contradiction. Doctrinal tension within a canon is not unique to the deuterocanon.

**Rebuttal 1:** The circularity is partial. The claim is not that Protestant readings of the protocanon are self-evidently correct, but that adding books that introduce new doctrinal content raises the bar for evidence.

**Rebuttal 2:** Internal tensions within the protocanon are real but operate within an established canonical context. The deuterocanon introduces claims from outside that canon.

---

## Pressure Points for Exclusion

### Argument 1: The Septuagint Was the Church's Bible

The strongest pressure on the exclusion position is that the early church used the Septuagint. If the church's operative Bible included the deuterocanon from its earliest period, then the Protestant decision to exclude these books is a rupture with practice rather than a restoration of origins. The Reformers were appealing to a Jewish canon that had itself undergone post-Christian standardization.

> **P1:** The early church used the Septuagint, which includes the deuterocanon.
>
> **P2:** The Masoretic Text reflects a standardization that was finalized after the apostolic period.
>
> **P3:** The Reformers' appeal to the Hebrew canon was in part an appeal to a post-apostolic Jewish text tradition.
>
> **C:** The exclusion position rests on a text base that postdates the early church's own canon.

**Critique 1:** The Masoretic Text preserves a tradition that predates its written standardization. The underlying Hebrew text was not invented in the medieval period; it was stabilized in writing.

**Critique 2:** The Reformers were not simply deferring to medieval Jewish scholarship. They were recovering the original language of the Old Testament and the canon recognized in Judaism before and during the time of Christ.

**Rebuttal 1:** The underlying tradition point is valid but does not resolve the question. The issue is which books belong in the canon, and the Hebrew tradition behind the Masoretic Text consistently excluded the deuterocanon.

**Rebuttal 2:** The timing of standardization matters less than the continuity of the tradition being standardized.

### Argument 2: Jerome's Preference Has Not Been Adequately Answered

Jerome, who produced the Vulgate and had direct access to Hebrew manuscripts and Jewish scholars, preferred the Hebrew canon. His reasons were philological and exegetical, not merely traditional. The Catholic Church's eventual rejection of his preference in favor of the broader canon was a theological decision, not a textual one. That decision shaped Western Christianity until the Reformation challenged it.

> **P1:** Jerome argued for the Hebrew canon on textual and scholarly grounds.
>
> **P2:** The Church's preference for the broader canon over Jerome's position reflected theological priorities rather than philological ones.
>
> **P3:** The Reformers were recovering a principled scholarly position with early patristic support, not simply innovating.
>
> **C:** The exclusion position has deeper roots in the tradition than the inclusion narrative typically acknowledges.

**Critique 1:** Jerome's minority position was overridden by the broader consensus of the Western church, including Augustine. Consensus has some evidential weight even if it is not decisive.

**Critique 2:** Jerome's own practice in the Vulgate included the deuterocanonical books even when he personally doubted their status, which complicates using him as a clean authority for exclusion.

**Rebuttal 1:** The consensus point is real, but consensus under Augustine's influence is not the same as undisputed tradition. The argument from majority is weaker when the majority view was not universal.

**Rebuttal 2:** Jerome's practice of including the books while doubting their authority shows that the question was already treated as a matter of received tradition versus scholarly judgment. The Reformers resolved it in the direction of scholarship.

---

# The Actual Hinge Points

Three questions control most of the dispute.

First, which text base defines the Old Testament canon: the Hebrew tradition behind the Masoretic Text, or the Septuagint received by the early church? The answer to this question does most of the work. If the Jewish canon before and during the time of Christ is the controlling criterion, the deuterocanon falls outside it. If the early church's actual operative Bible is the controlling criterion, the deuterocanon belongs in it.

Second, does doctrinal novelty count as evidence against canonicity? The deuterocanon introduces passages on prayer for the dead and meritorious almsgiving that have no clear parallel in the protocanon. If a book can be canonical while introducing doctrines not otherwise present in Scripture, these passages are not disqualifying. If canonical books must cohere doctrinally with an already-established witness, they are a serious problem.

Third, was the pre-Reformation Western canon a settled tradition or a contested one? The inclusion position requires that Trent ratified rather than created the Catholic canon. The exclusion position requires that the Reformers recovered a prior tradition rather than inventing one. Both claims are partly true, which is what makes this hinge genuinely open.
