## Part One: Debate Outline

Two questions control most of the dispute over the deuterocanon.

First, what text base defines the Old Testament canon: the Masoretic Text or the Septuagint?

Second, must canonical books match the human author's conscious original intent, or can later revelation disclose a fuller sense that remains consistent with the earlier text?

These hinges map onto the earlier disputes about prophecy fulfillment and typology.

## Domain & Evidence

The domain is Old Testament canon boundaries and the status of the deuterocanonical books.

Evidence includes:

- The Masoretic Text, which excludes the deuterocanon
- The Septuagint, which includes them
- Dead Sea Scrolls, which contain some deuterocanonical books
- Early church usage of the Septuagint
- Council of Trent (1546), which formally affirmed the deuterocanon as canonical for the Catholic Church
- Protestant confessions post-Reformation, which exclude them from the canon

## Key Definitions

**Deuterocanon**: Books included in the Septuagint but not in the Masoretic Text. Also called Apocrypha by Protestants. The term means "belonging to a second canon."

**Masoretic Text**: The standardized Hebrew text of the Old Testament, finalized by Jewish scribes around the 7th–10th centuries CE. Forms the basis for the Protestant Old Testament.

**Septuagint**: The Greek translation of the Old Testament, produced around the 3rd–2nd centuries BCE. Includes the deuterocanonical books and was the primary Old Testament text used by the early church.

**Apocrypha**: Literally "hidden." Protestant term for the deuterocanonical books, often implying they are not Scripture.

**Canon**: The list of books received as authoritative Scripture.

## Foundational Assumptions

**Assumption 1**: The Masoretic Text represents the authentic Jewish canon.

If meaning is fixed by what the Jewish community recognized as Scripture before Christ, the deuterocanon falls outside the canon.

**Assumption 2**: The Septuagint represents the canonical text used by Christ and the early church.

If the early church's text base defines the Old Testament for Christians, the deuterocanon belongs in the canon.

**Assumption 3**: Canonical status depends on divine authority, not human authorial intent.

If a book can carry divine authority even when the human author did not consciously intend canonical status, the deuterocanon can be Scripture.

**Assumption 4**: Doctrinal clarity requires a fixed, narrow canon.

If adding books risks doctrinal confusion or error, exclusion is the safer path.

## Position A: Include the Deuterocanon as Scripture

The Catholic and Orthodox churches receive the deuterocanon as fully canonical.

### Premises

1. The Septuagint was the Old Testament text used by Christ and the apostles.

2. The early church consistently used the Septuagint and its broader canon.

3. The Catholic Church affirmed the deuterocanon at the Council of Trent.

4. The Dead Sea Scrolls show these books were valued in pre-Christian Jewish communities.

5. Doctrine supported by the deuterocanon (prayer for the dead, justification by works, etc.) is coherent with overall Scripture.

### Implications

- The Old Testament canon is broader than the Masoretic Text.

- The deuterocanon carries the same authority as the protocanon.

- Catholic doctrines like prayer for the dead and merit gain additional textual support.

- The Protestant claim that the deuterocanon was "arbitrarily added" at Trent is historically inaccurate.

## Position B: Exclude the Deuterocanon from the Canon

The Protestant church excludes the deuterocanon from the canonical Old Testament.

### Premises

1. The Masoretic Text represents the authentic Jewish canon recognized before Christ.

2. The Jewish community excluded the deuterocanon from their Scripture.

3. The deuterocanon contains doctrines not found in the protocanon (e.g., prayer for the dead).

4. Some deuterocanonical passages rely on textual errors or questionable history.

5. Protestant confessions after the Reformation consistently exclude them.

### Implications

- The Old Testament canon matches the Masoretic Text.

- The deuterocanon is useful for reading but not authoritative for doctrine.

- Doctrines supported only by the deuterocanon lack canonical grounding.

- The Reformers restored the original Jewish canon rather than inventing a new one.

## Hinges

**Hinge 1**: Text base for the Old Testament

- Include: Septuagint represents the canonical text for Christians.

- Exclude: Masoretic Text represents the authentic Jewish canon.

**Hinge 2**: Canonical authority and authorial intent

- Include: A book can be Scripture even if the human author did not consciously intend canonical status.

- Exclude: Canonical status requires alignment with what the recognizing community accepted as Scripture.

**Hinge 3**: Doctrinal continuity

- Include: The deuterocanon supports doctrines coherent with the broader biblical witness.

- Exclude: The deuterocanon introduces doctrines absent from the protocanon.
