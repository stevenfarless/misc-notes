## Domain & Evidence

Both positions affirm the incarnation, the deity of Christ, the genuine humanity of Christ, the atoning death and bodily resurrection of Christ, and the unique saving significance of his person and work.

The dispute concerns the metaphysical and exegetical account of the incarnation. Chalcedonian Christology holds that the one person of the Son assumed a complete human nature while remaining fully divine, so that Christ is one person in two natures, divine and human, without confusion, change, division, or separation. Kenotic Christology holds that Philippians 2 describes a real self-emptying in which the Son voluntarily relinquished, suspended, or did not exercise some aspect of divine prerogative in order to become genuinely human.

This debate matters because every account of Christ's mediation, priesthood, temptation, obedience, death, and resurrection depends on it. If Christ's human limitations are only appearances explained by appeal to the divine nature, then his humanity risks becoming thin. If Christ truly gave up or bracketed divine attributes, the immutability and fullness of deity become difficult to maintain. The pressure point is the same on both sides: how to preserve both full deity and full humanity without letting one swallow the other.

The live disagreements are specific: what Philippians 2:5-11 means by *ekenosen* (emptied himself), how to interpret statements of Jesus' ignorance, growth, fatigue, and limitation, whether divine attributes can be surrendered or only non-independently exercised, and whether Chalcedon's formula is a faithful summary of the biblical data or a conceptual shield that leaves the hard questions unresolved.

## Key Definitions

### Chalcedonian Christology

**Position A, Chalcedonian Christology:** the Definition of Chalcedon (451) teaches that the one and same Son, our Lord Jesus Christ, is to be acknowledged in two natures, without confusion, without change, without division, and without separation. The divine nature remains fully divine. The human nature remains fully human. The union is personal rather than essential: the natures are united in the one person of the Son.

**Position B, Kenotic Christology:** Chalcedon's formula protects orthodoxy at the boundary level, but often leaves the mechanism of incarnation underdescribed. In practice, it can function as a way of repeating that Christ is fully God and fully man without explaining how that works in the concrete gospel data of ignorance, suffering, temptation, and growth.

### Kenotic Christology

**Position B, Kenotic Christology:** in the incarnation the Son genuinely emptied himself as described in Philippians 2:7. That self-emptying is not merely addition of a human nature but some real renunciation, suspension, concealment, or non-use of divine prerogatives in the incarnate state. Different kenotic models vary. Some argue the Son surrendered the independent exercise of omniscience, omnipotence, or omnipresence. Others argue he retained all divine attributes but accepted the conditions of human consciousness such that divine knowledge was not available to his human experience except as the Spirit mediated it.

**Position A, Chalcedonian Christology:** any account that has the Son giving up divine attributes compromises divine immutability and full deity. God cannot cease to possess what it is to be God. The safer and more coherent account is that the Son assumed a human nature and lived a truly human life through that nature without subtraction from the divine nature.

### Communicatio Idiomatum

**Position A:** the communicatio idiomatum means that the properties of each nature may be predicated of the one person. The person who is God can be said to suffer and die according to the human nature; the person who is man can be said to be omnipotent according to the divine nature. This does not mix the natures or transfer divine attributes into the human nature. It safeguards the unity of the person while preserving the distinction of natures.

**Position B:** the concept is useful, but in many presentations it becomes a verbal solution that explains away the force of the gospel data. Saying that Christ was ignorant according to the human nature but omniscient according to the divine nature can leave the concrete question untouched: what was the consciousness of the incarnate Son actually like as he lived the human life narrated in the Gospels?

### Extra Calvinisticum

**Position A:** the extra Calvinisticum is the claim that the Son, even in the incarnation, is not exhaustively contained within the assumed human nature. The Logos is united to the humanity of Christ but also remains beyond it, sustaining the universe according to the divine nature. This prevents collapsing the divine life into the earthly life of Jesus and protects the transcendence of the Son.

**Position B:** the extra Calvinisticum can make the incarnation look split-level, as if the Logos is living one life beyond the humanity and another within it. The concern is that this weakens the existential unity of the incarnate life presented in the Gospels.

## Foundational Assumptions

### Position A assumes

Chalcedonian Christology begins with **the immutability and fullness of deity**. If the Son is truly God, he cannot cease to possess divine attributes in the incarnation. Omniscience, omnipotence, and eternality are not accessories that can be removed temporarily. They belong to what God is. The incarnation therefore must be understood as addition rather than subtraction: the Son assumes a human nature without any loss to the divine nature.

A further assumption is that **the church's conciliar boundaries were driven by the need to preserve the biblical data against reduction in either direction**. Arius compromised deity. Apollinaris compromised humanity. Eutyches compromised distinction. Nestorius compromised unity. Chalcedon represents the church's hard-won judgment that the biblical witness forces all four boundaries at once.

### Position B assumes

Kenotic Christology begins with **the seriousness of the Gospel portrayals of limitation**. Jesus grows in wisdom (Luke 2:52), learns obedience through suffering (Hebrews 5:8), does not know the day or hour (Mark 13:32), becomes tired, hungry, and sorrowful. These are not incidental narrative details. They are central to the presentation of Christ's human life.

A further assumption is that **full humanity requires more than possession of a human body and a human psychology attached to a fully functioning divine consciousness behind the scenes**. If the incarnate Son always has immediate access to omniscience and omnipotence at the personal level, then the human life risks becoming an instrument through which a divine subject only appears to live under creaturely limits. Kenotic proposals try to prevent that outcome.

# Position A: Chalcedonian Christology: Strongest Case

---

## Argument 1: The New Testament Requires Full Deity and Full Humanity Without Mixture

The New Testament speaks of Christ in ways that require both full deity and full humanity. John 1:1 identifies the Word as God. John 1:14 says the Word became flesh. Colossians 2:9 states that in him the fullness of deity dwells bodily. Hebrews 2:14 says he shared in flesh and blood. Any adequate Christology must preserve both sets of claims without collapsing one into the other. Chalcedon's two-natures formula does precisely this by distinguishing nature from person and insisting that the one person of the Son subsists in both a complete divine nature and a complete human nature.

> **P1:** The New Testament attributes full deity to Christ in texts such as John 1:1 and Colossians 2:9.
>
> **P2:** The New Testament attributes full humanity to Christ in texts such as John 1:14 and Hebrews 2:14.
>
> **P3:** A coherent Christology must preserve both full deity and full humanity without reduction.
>
> **C:** Chalcedon's one-person-two-natures formula remains the most stable account of the biblical data because it protects both sets of claims at once.

## Argument 2: Divine Attributes Cannot Be Surrendered Without Ceasing To Be God

If the Son surrenders omniscience, omnipotence, or omnipresence in the incarnation, the problem is not merely functional but ontological. A being who lacks those attributes is not fully God. Theism does not treat divine attributes as detachable capacities but as constitutive perfections of God's being. The Son cannot become less than fully divine in order to become incarnate. The incarnation is therefore not a matter of subtraction from deity but of assumption of humanity.

> **P1:** Divine attributes such as omniscience and omnipotence are constitutive of deity rather than optional functions.
>
> **P2:** If the Son ceases to possess constitutive divine attributes, he ceases to be fully God.
>
> **P3:** The New Testament and catholic tradition insist that the incarnate Son remains fully God.
>
> **C:** Kenotic models involving surrender of divine attributes are incompatible with full deity; the incarnation must be understood as addition of humanity rather than subtraction from deity.

## Argument 3: Philippians 2 Describes Humiliation by Addition, Not Divestment

Philippians 2:6-8 says that Christ, being in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be exploited, but emptied himself by taking the form of a servant and being born in the likeness of men. The grammar points to the manner of emptying: he emptied himself **by taking**, not by giving up. The self-emptying is the assumption of servant form and the path of obedient humiliation unto death. The text's stress lies on status reversal and obedient descent, not on metaphysical surrender of divine attributes.

> **P1:** Philippians 2 explains Christ's self-emptying with participles of addition: taking the form of a servant and being born in human likeness.
>
> **P2:** The immediate context emphasizes humiliation, obedience, and death rather than metaphysical change in the divine nature.
>
> **P3:** Reading *ekenosen* as subtraction imports a metaphysical claim the text does not explicitly make.
>
> **C:** Philippians 2 is best read as humiliation by assumption of lowly human form rather than relinquishment of divine attributes.

## Argument 4: The Ignorance Texts Are Best Read Through the Distinction of Natures

Texts like Mark 13:32, where the Son does not know the day or hour, are real and must not be minimized. Chalcedonian Christology handles them by distinguishing what belongs to the human nature from what belongs to the divine nature while attributing both to the one person. Christ truly did not know according to his human mind. At the same time, as the divine Son he remains omniscient. This is not evasion but the necessary implication of affirming two complete natures. A complete human nature includes a finite human intellect. If Christ lacked a finite human intellect, he would not be fully human.

> **P1:** Mark 13:32 presents a genuine instance of ignorance in Christ.
>
> **P2:** Full humanity requires a finite human intellect capable of limited knowledge.
>
> **P3:** Full deity requires omniscience.
>
> **C:** The two-natures distinction explains how the one person can truly experience human ignorance without surrendering divine omniscience.

---

## Position A -- Critique of Position B

### Critique 1: Kenotic Christology Risks Ontological Reduction of Deity

The central problem with strong kenotic proposals is that they relocate limitation from the assumed humanity to the divine person as such. Once the Son as person gives up omniscience or omnipotence, the problem is no longer about incarnation but about change in God. That makes the incarnation a modification of deity rather than the assumption of humanity, and the result is a Son who is no longer consubstantial with the Father in the classical sense.

> **P1:** Strong kenotic models place limitation at the level of the divine person rather than only at the level of the assumed human nature.
>
> **P2:** Limitation at the level of the divine person implies change in what the Son is as God.
>
> **P3:** Change in what the Son is as God compromises consubstantiality and immutability.
>
> **C:** Strong kenotic Christology risks reducing or altering deity in order to secure humanity, which is too high a doctrinal price.

### Critique 2: Kenotic Christology Lacks a Stable Stopping Point

Once one says the Son surrendered or suspended one divine attribute in the incarnation, the question becomes why not more. If omniscience may be bracketed, why not eternality. If omnipotence may be non-operative, how is providence maintained. If omnipresence is surrendered, what sustains the universe. The logic invites progressive contraction of deity without a clear principled boundary.

> **P1:** Kenotic theories permit some divine prerogatives to be suspended, concealed, or relinquished.
>
> **P2:** No obvious internal principle identifies which divine attributes may be suspended without compromising deity.
>
> **P3:** The result is a potentially unstable doctrine with no clear stopping point.
>
> **C:** Kenotic Christology lacks a sufficiently stable principle for distinguishing permissible incarnation-related limitation from impermissible reduction of deity.

### Critique 3: The Biblical Pattern Includes Manifestations of Divine Authority During the Incarnate Life

Jesus forgives sins, commands nature, knows hearts, receives worship, and exercises authority over demons and death. These are not occasional leaks from a surrendered deity but signs that the incarnate Christ remains who he eternally is. Even when one grants that he lives in dependence on the Spirit, the pattern is not one of a merely Spirit-anointed human prophet. The divine identity remains active throughout the Gospel narratives.

> **P1:** The Gospels present Christ exercising divine prerogatives including forgiving sins and commanding creation.
>
> **P2:** These acts are integral to his identity and mission, not marginal anomalies.
>
> **P3:** A Christology centered on relinquishment struggles to account for the continued presence of these divine manifestations.
>
> **C:** The Gospel data fit better with continuing full deity in the incarnate life than with any model centered on surrender of divine attributes.

---

## Position A -- Rebuttals to Position B

### Rebuttal 1: Taking the Humanity Seriously Does Not Require Subtracting from Deity

Kenotic theologians often frame the issue as a choice between real humanity and intact deity. Chalcedonian Christology rejects the premise. A complete human nature includes a human body, will, affections, and intellect. Christ can therefore be genuinely tired, ignorant according to his human mind, tempted, sorrowful, and obedient without any subtraction from the divine nature. The humanity is real because it is complete, not because deity is reduced.

> **P1:** Full humanity requires completeness of the human nature, not reduction of the divine nature.
>
> **P2:** A complete human nature can account for Christ's growth, limitation, suffering, and obedience.
>
> **P3:** Therefore the kenotic move is unnecessary to secure genuine humanity.
>
> **C:** Chalcedon can take the Gospel data with full seriousness without subtracting from deity.

### Rebuttal 2: The Charge of Verbal Evasion Misreads the Work of the Distinctions

Kenotic critics argue that phrases like "according to the human nature" merely postpone the question. But all serious metaphysical explanations use distinctions to prevent contradiction. The distinction between person and nature is not a verbal patch. It is the conceptual tool required by the biblical data themselves. Without it, one is left either with a merely human Christ empowered by God, or with a divine being only appearing human.

> **P1:** The biblical data require affirming things of Christ that would otherwise be contradictory.
>
> **P2:** The person-nature distinction is the conceptual means by which those affirmations are rendered coherent.
>
> **P3:** Removing the distinction does not solve the problem; it intensifies it.
>
> **C:** The Chalcedonian distinctions are not evasions but the minimum conceptual equipment required to preserve the full range of biblical claims.

# Position B: Kenotic Christology: Strongest Case

---

## Argument 1: Philippians 2 Describes a Real Self-Emptying

The language of Philippians 2 is stronger than a simple addition formula suggests. Paul does not merely say that Christ took servant form; he says Christ emptied himself. The ordinary force of the verb indicates some real renunciation. The humiliation is not only social status reversal. It concerns the mode of existence the Son accepts in the incarnation. Kenotic theology argues that taking servant form is the concrete expression of that self-emptying, not a denial that any real emptying occurred.

> **P1:** Philippians 2 explicitly says Christ emptied himself.
>
> **P2:** The ordinary force of the verb suggests a real renunciation rather than a purely formal addition.
>
> **P3:** The servant form and human likeness are the concrete expression of that emptying, not a denial of it.
>
> **C:** Philippians 2 provides positive exegetical warrant for some real self-limitation in the incarnate life of the Son.

## Argument 2: The Gospel Portrait of Jesus Includes More Than Merely Human-Locatable Limitations

Jesus does not simply have a human body with ordinary creaturely sensations. He lives a life of dependence, prayer, growth, temptation, and non-omniscient consciousness. He says the Son does not know the day or hour. He prays rather than acts as one simply drawing from inherent omnipotence. He is led by the Spirit into the wilderness and empowered by the Spirit in ministry. Kenotic theologians argue that this pattern is best explained by genuine self-limitation in the incarnate mode of life rather than by appeal to a fully active omniscient divine consciousness operating behind a finite human consciousness.

> **P1:** The Gospels portray Jesus as living in dependence, prayer, growth, and finite knowledge.
>
> **P2:** This pattern is central to his incarnate life rather than incidental.
>
> **P3:** A kenotic account explains the pattern by positing genuine self-limitation in the incarnate life.
>
> **C:** The Gospel portrait fits more naturally with some real self-limitation than with a model in which omniscience remains fully available at the personal level throughout.

## Argument 3: A Non-Kenotic Model Risks Functional Docetism

If the incarnate Son always retains full immediate access to omniscience and omnipotence at the personal level, then the human limitations narrated in the Gospels can seem performative rather than structural. He appears to live a human life while possessing behind the scenes the resources of deity in full active availability. Kenotic Christology aims to prevent that split by insisting that the incarnation involved entering fully into the conditions of creaturely life.

> **P1:** Full active access to omniscience and omnipotence at the personal level risks making Christ's limitations appear contingent or staged.
>
> **P2:** The Gospel narratives present Christ's limitations as integral to his real human existence.
>
> **P3:** Kenotic Christology secures the depth of Christ's solidarity with humanity by positing real self-limitation.
>
> **C:** Kenotic Christology avoids a functionally docetic reading of the incarnation better than many non-kenotic presentations do.

## Argument 4: The Incarnation Is an Act of Divine Humility at the Level of Mode of Existence

The incarnation is not only the addition of a human nature while everything else remains straightforwardly intact at the level of lived existence. It is the Son's act of entering into the vulnerability, dependence, and lowliness of creaturely life. Kenotic Christology argues that divine humility in the incarnation should be taken with full seriousness. A merely additive model can preserve ontological orthodoxy but under-describe the depth of the condescension.

> **P1:** The New Testament presents the incarnation as the supreme act of divine humility.
>
> **P2:** Humility in this context concerns not only external appearance but actual mode of life under creaturely conditions.
>
> **P3:** A kenotic account gives doctrinal content to that mode of humble existence.
>
> **C:** Kenotic Christology captures the existential depth of the incarnation more fully than a merely additive model.

## Position B -- Critique of Position A

### Critique 1: Chalcedon Often Functions as a Boundary Formula Rather Than an Explanatory Account

The classic formula tells us what must not be said: no confusion, no change, no division, no separation. That is valuable. But when one asks how the incarnate Son actually lived under creaturely limits, the formula often yields only restatement. Christ is ignorant according to one nature and omniscient according to the other. Christ suffers according to one nature and impassibly sustains the universe according to the other. The concern is that the formula protects mystery by leaving the lived unity of the incarnate life conceptually underdeveloped.

> **P1:** Chalcedon's language is primarily boundary-setting rather than mechanism-explaining.
>
> **P2:** The lived unity of Christ's incarnate experience remains difficult to describe using the formula alone.
>
> **P3:** Repetition of the distinction does not always answer the concrete questions raised by the Gospel data.
>
> **C:** Chalcedon is necessary as a boundary but often insufficient as an explanatory account of the incarnation's lived reality.

### Critique 2: The Two-Natures Distinction Can Become Existentially Split-Level

A recurring concern in non-kenotic Christology is that the incarnate life begins to look layered. At one level Christ is finite, tired, and ignorant. At another level the same personal subject remains omniscient, omnipotent, and sustaining all things. The concern is not formal contradiction but existential plausibility. What was it like for the incarnate Son to live such a life, and how does this avoid a dual-track consciousness that the Gospel narratives themselves do not describe.

> **P1:** Non-kenotic models often describe Christ as finite in one respect and fully omniscient in another.
>
> **P2:** This can generate a split-level picture of consciousness and agency in the incarnate life.
>
> **P3:** The Gospel narratives do not present Jesus as operating with that kind of explicit dual-track self-awareness.
>
> **C:** Chalcedonian accounts risk becoming existentially split-level in a way that weakens the coherence of Christ's lived human life.

---

## Position B -- Rebuttals to Position A

### Rebuttal 1: Not All Kenotic Models Surrender Attributes Ontologically

Chalcedonian critics often target the strongest forms of kenoticism, those in which the Son literally gives up divine attributes. But many modern kenotic models are more careful. They argue for non-use, concealment, or refracted exercise through the conditions of human consciousness rather than ontological divestment. The point is not that God stopped being God. The point is that the Son chose to live the incarnate life without drawing straightforwardly on the full range of divine prerogatives in ordinary human experience.

> **P1:** Modern kenotic theories vary and do not all posit literal loss of divine attributes.
>
> **P2:** Some models speak of voluntary non-use or concealment rather than ontological surrender.
>
> **P3:** Those models aim to preserve full deity while still taking the Gospel limitations seriously.
>
> **C:** The standard Chalcedonian critique often targets the strongest kenotic versions and does not always engage the more nuanced contemporary proposals.

### Rebuttal 2: The Divine Acts in the Gospels Do Not Settle the Mechanism Question

Jesus' miracles, authority over demons, and forgiveness of sins prove that he is no mere prophet. But they do not by themselves determine how the incarnate life is structured. Kenotic theologians can affirm that Christ acts with unique divine authority while still holding that his incarnate mode of consciousness and dependence involved voluntary self-limitation. The presence of divine acts does not prove that no kenotic restraint was operative.

> **P1:** The Gospel narratives show Christ exercising divine authority.
>
> **P2:** Those acts do not on their own specify whether the incarnate life involved voluntary restraint in other respects.
>
> **P3:** Therefore the divine manifestations do not by themselves refute nuanced kenotic models.
>
> **C:** The miraculous and authoritative acts of Jesus establish his identity but do not settle the debate over the mode of incarnate self-limitation.

# The Three Actual Hinges

The first hinge is **what counts as a real reading of Philippians 2**. Chalcedonian Christology reads the self-emptying as humiliation by addition. Kenotic Christology reads it as requiring some real renunciation or self-limitation. The dispute is not over whether Christ humbled himself. Both sides affirm that. The dispute is whether the humility is exhausted by assuming servant form or whether the text presses toward a deeper account of incarnate limitation.

The second hinge is **how to interpret the ignorance and limitation texts**. Chalcedonian Christology locates these in the completeness of the human nature. Kenotic Christology argues that this can leave the personal subject insufficiently integrated at the experiential level. The real question is not whether Christ had a human mind. Both sides agree that he did. The question is whether the one person of the Son lived the incarnation under conditions of genuine personal self-limitation.

The third hinge is **whether immutability rules out all strong forms of kenosis**. If divine attributes are constitutive and non-negotiable, any surrender model is excluded at once. If one distinguishes ontological possession from incarnate mode of exercise, more nuanced kenotic models become possible. The debate then turns on whether that distinction is coherent or merely a softer form of the same problem.
