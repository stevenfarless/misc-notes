# Theism vs. Atheism

## Scope of the Debate

The question is whether any god or gods exist — specifically, for Christian theism, whether a personal, omnipotent, omniscient, morally perfect being exists who is the cause and sustainer of the universe. Atheism is the denial of this (or any) such being. Agnosticism claims the question is undecided or undecidable and is orthogonal to the theism/atheism axis.

## Classical Arguments for Theism

**Cosmological arguments** (Aquinas's Five Ways, Leibniz's contingency argument, Kalam) move from the existence or contingency of the universe to a necessary or uncaused cause. The Kalam version: (1) Everything that begins to exist has a cause; (2) The universe began to exist; (3) Therefore the universe has a cause external to itself. Atheist objections target premise (2) via quantum cosmology (Hawking, Vilenkin) or challenge whether "cause" applies outside spacetime.

**Teleological arguments** (Paley, Fine-Tuning) argue that the apparent design or improbable precision of physical constants (e.g., cosmological constant, strong nuclear force) indicates intentional calibration. The fine-tuning argument is the most technically developed form, using the low prior probability of life-permitting constants under a chance hypothesis. Multiverse hypotheses are the standard naturalist response, but they introduce their own explanatory costs.

**Moral arguments** (C.S. Lewis, Kant, contemporary: Craig) argue from the existence of objective moral facts to a moral lawgiver. The argument requires the premise that moral realism is true — that moral claims are genuinely true or false independent of human opinion. Error theorists (Mackie) reject the premise; naturalist moral realists (Railton, Parfit) accept moral realism but deny it entails theism.

**Ontological arguments** (Anselm, Plantinga's modal version) are purely a priori. Plantinga's version: If it is possible that a maximally great being exists, then such a being exists in all possible worlds, including the actual world. The argument is valid but controversial — critics challenge whether "maximal greatness" is coherent or whether possibility licensing the conclusion can be granted without begging the question.

## Principal Atheist Arguments

**The Problem of Evil** remains the most widely discussed argument against theism. The logical version (Mackie, Flew) holds that an omnipotent, omniscient, omnibenevolent God is logically incompatible with the existence of any evil. Most philosophers now consider this version defeated by Alvin Plantinga's free will defense for moral evil. The evidential version (Rowe, Draper) is weaker in form but harder to dismiss: given the quantity and distribution of gratuitous suffering (especially in animals, before any human moral agent existed), the probability of classical theism is reduced. Theist responses include skeptical theism (Wykstra, Bergmann): finite minds have no reliable access to what omniscient reasons God might have for permitting specific evils.

**The Hiddenness of God** (J.L. Schellenberg) argues that a perfectly loving God would ensure that all non-resistant nonbelievers come to belief in him, but nonbelief clearly exists. Theist responses dispute either that divine love entails that obligation or that all nonbelief is genuinely "non-resistant."

**The Argument from Inconsistent Revelations** notes that mutually exclusive religious traditions each claim divine authorization. This is more of a challenge to exclusive religious claims than to bare theism.

## Methodological Notes

The debate operates at the intersection of analytic philosophy of religion, natural theology, and epistemology. Key figures: Plantinga (Reformed Epistemology — belief in God as "properly basic," not requiring inferential justification), Swinburne (Bayesian cumulative case), J.L. Mackie and Graham Oppy (atheism), John Schellenberg (agnostic/atheist).

Reformed Epistemology is relevant here because it bypasses natural theology entirely: theistic belief can be warranted without arguments, functioning like perceptual or memorial beliefs. Critics (Mackie, Martin) argue this licenses any belief and provides no way to adjudicate competing religious claims.
