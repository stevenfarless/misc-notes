## Domain & Evidence

Both positions affirm that Scripture is the word of God, that it is divinely inspired in a sense that distinguishes it from all other literature, that it carries unique authority over Christian faith and practice, and that Jesus Christ treated the Old Testament Scriptures as authoritative and trustworthy. Both reject the liberal position that Scripture is merely a human religious document with no special divine origin.

The dispute is internal to evangelical and conservative Protestant scholarship. It concerns the scope and nature of Scripture's inerrancy: whether the absence of error extends to everything Scripture affirms (including incidental historical, geographical, and scientific details) or whether it extends only to what Scripture intends to teach regarding salvation, theology, and ethics.

This document is the methodological foundation for every other debate in this repo. Every argument in the Soteriology, Eschatology, Anthropology, Theology Proper, and Ecclesiology documents bottoms out in a claim about what a text says, what it intends to affirm, and how confidently the interpreter can trust its details. The inerrancy question determines how much epistemic weight a historical or factual detail in a text can carry in an argument, and whether apparent contradictions or errors between texts constitute genuine defeaters for a theological conclusion.

The live disagreements are specific: what *theopneustos* (God-breathed, 2 Timothy 3:16) entails about the product of inspiration, how to interpret the phenomena of Scripture (numerical discrepancies, divergent parallel accounts, apparent historical errors), whether the autographs-only limitation on inerrancy is a meaningful or a vacuous claim, whether the Rogers-McKim thesis about the historical tradition is historically accurate, and how to handle the distinction between what Scripture affirms and what it merely reports or assumes.

## Key Definitions

### Plenary Inerrancy

**Position A, plenary inerrancy:** Scripture, in its original autographs, is without error in everything it affirms, because it is the product of divine inspiration extending to the words themselves (verbal plenary inspiration). Inerrancy is not limited to matters of faith and practice; it extends to historical, geographical, and scientific assertions where Scripture makes them. The Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy (1978), signed by nearly 300 evangelical scholars, is the definitive modern formulation. Article XII states: "We affirm that Scripture in its entirety is inerrant, being free from all falsehood, fraud, or deceit. We deny that Biblical infallibility and inerrancy are limited to spiritual, religious, or redemptive themes, exclusive of assertions in the fields of history and science."

**Position B, limited inerrancy:** plenary inerrancy imposes on Scripture a standard of precision that the biblical authors did not intend and that the phenomena of Scripture do not support; it forces harmonistic interpretations that are implausible and creates an apologetic liability when the harmonizations fail.

### Limited Inerrancy / Infallibility

**Position B, limited inerrancy:** Scripture is fully authoritative and without error in everything it intends to teach regarding salvation, theology, and ethics. Its truthfulness is functional and purposive: the Bible accomplishes without failure the purpose for which God inspired it. Incidental details of history, geography, numbers, and science may reflect the knowledge conventions of the human authors' time and culture without undermining the Bible's theological authority. I. Howard Marshall's formulation is representative: the Bible is infallible in the sense that it unfailingly achieves its saving purpose, but inerrancy in the Chicago Statement sense overreaches what inspiration guarantees.

**Position A, plenary inerrancy:** the limited inerrancy position provides no principled boundary between incidental errors and theological errors; the distinction between what Scripture intends to teach and what it merely assumes or reports is determined by the interpreter, not by the text, which gives the interpreter authority to exempt any inconvenient assertion from the scope of Scripture's truthfulness.

### Verbal Plenary Inspiration (Mechanism Question)

**The mechanism position held by Position A:** divine inspiration extends to the words of Scripture, not merely to the concepts, ideas, or experiences of the human authors. God superintended the human authors such that the words they wrote were exactly what he intended, without overriding their personalities, vocabularies, or literary styles. This is the mechanism that plenary inerrancy requires: if inspiration extended only to concepts and the authors chose their own words, the words could misrepresent the concepts, and inerrancy of the text would not follow from inspiration of the authors.

**Position B critique:** verbal plenary inspiration as strict dictation conflicts with the evident human authorship of Scripture: the differences in style between Paul and John, the Synoptic variations, the Septuagint quotations in the New Testament that diverge from the Hebrew Masoretic text, and the literary conventions of the genres used. A more adequate account of inspiration holds that God worked through human authors fully, including their knowledge, assumptions, and literary conventions, producing a text that is fully divine and fully human without requiring that every word be individually selected by direct divine dictation.

**Position A response:** verbal plenary inspiration is not dictation theory. The authors were not passive instruments; God worked through their personalities, researches, memories, and literary choices to produce exactly what he intended. The result is a text whose words are the words God intended without bypassing the human authors' genuine agency. The differences between Paul and John reflect different human authors; verbal inspiration means God's intention was expressed through those different voices, not that every word was dictated identically.

### The Autographs Limitation

**Position A:** inerrancy applies to the original autographs, the manuscripts produced by the biblical authors, not to any subsequent copy or translation. This is not a retreat from inerrancy; it is a precise statement of its scope. Warfield argued that textual criticism can reconstruct the text with sufficient accuracy to support the doctrine, and that confidence in the text's general reliability is compatible with acknowledging that copyists introduced errors. The autographs limitation was not invented to protect inerrancy from embarrassment; it reflects the straightforward fact that inspiration attaches to the act of divinely superintended authorship, not to the subsequent process of transmission.

**Position B:** the autographs limitation renders inerrancy practically unfalsifiable and therefore theologically vacuous. No autograph exists or is likely ever to be recovered. Any apparent error in any extant manuscript can always be attributed to copyist error rather than to the original text. A doctrine that can never be tested against any accessible evidence is not doing the epistemic work that a doctrine of Scripture's truthfulness is supposed to do.

## Foundational Assumptions

### Position A assumes

Plenary inerrantists begin with **the theological entailment from inspiration to inerrancy**. If God is the ultimate author of Scripture and God cannot lie or err (Numbers 23:19; Titus 1:2; Hebrews 6:18), then whatever God produces as his word cannot contain error. The argument is deductive: the character of the author determines the character of the product. If inspiration genuinely makes Scripture God's word in any meaningful sense, then Scripture shares the attribute of truthfulness that belongs to God himself. A Scripture that errs in historical details is a Scripture whose divine authorship is qualified in ways that undermine the theological weight of the claim that it is God's word.

A further assumption is that **the distinction between incidental and intentional assertions is not available to the interpreter without a principle external to the text**. If the interpreter decides which assertions Scripture intends to make and which are merely incidental, the interpreter rather than the text becomes the authority. The Chicago Statement's concern is precisely this: limiting inerrancy to intended theological assertions gives the interpreter a tool to exempt any inconvenient text from the scope of Scripture's authority.

### Position B assumes

Limited inerrantists begin with **the phenomena of Scripture as a data set that any adequate doctrine of inspiration must account for**. The biblical texts contain numerical discrepancies between parallel accounts, differences in the wording of the same speech across the Gospels, Old Testament quotations in the New Testament that diverge from the Hebrew text, apparent conflicts between biblical chronology and external historical evidence, and geocentric cosmological assumptions in the poetic and wisdom literature. These phenomena are not fabricated by critics; they are in the text. A doctrine of inerrancy that requires harmonizing every one of them is a doctrine under permanent apologetic pressure, and the harmonizations are sometimes less plausible than admitting a minor error.

A further assumption is that **the purpose of inspiration determines its scope**. God inspired Scripture to accomplish a specific purpose: to make human beings wise for salvation through faith in Christ (2 Timothy 3:15) and to equip them for every good work (3:17). The purpose is soteriological and ethical. Inerrancy in matters of ancient Near Eastern chronology or the number of soldiers in a given army is not necessary for that purpose, and the claim that inspiration guarantees it extends inspiration beyond its stated aim.

# Position A: Plenary Inerrancy: Strongest Case

---

## Argument 1: The Self-Witness of Scripture to Its Own Truthfulness

Scripture does not merely claim authority; it claims truth. Psalm 119:160 states: "The sum of your word is truth, and every one of your righteous rules endures forever." Proverbs 30:5 states: "Every word of God proves true." John 17:17 has Jesus praying: "Sanctify them in the truth; your word is truth." These are not claims that Scripture is true in a limited or functional sense; they are unqualified claims about Scripture's truth. The Psalmist does not say "the salvific portions of your word are truth" or "the theological substance of your word is truth"; he says the sum is truth and every word proves true. The self-witness of Scripture to its own truthfulness does not allow the interpreter to introduce a category of incidental error.

> **P1:** Psalm 119:160 and Proverbs 30:5 make unqualified claims about the truthfulness of God's word, without distinguishing salvific from incidental assertions.
>
> **P2:** John 17:17 identifies God's word with truth as such, not with a subset of truth limited to theological subject matter.
>
> **P3:** Introducing a category of incidental errors that fall outside the scope of Scripture's truthfulness requires overriding the unqualified scope of Scripture's own self-witness.
>
> **C:** Scripture's self-witness to its own truthfulness is unqualified; limiting inerrancy to intended theological assertions imports a distinction that the self-witness texts do not support.

## Argument 2: Jesus' Use of Scripture Treats Its Details as Authoritative

Jesus treats Old Testament texts with a precision that extends to individual words and grammatical details. In Matthew 22:31-32, his argument about the resurrection turns on the present tense of "I am the God of Abraham" rather than "I was." In John 10:35, he argues from the fact that the Old Testament called certain human judges "gods," and adds: "Scripture cannot be broken." In Matthew 5:18, he states that not one iota or dot will pass from the Law. These examples show that Jesus' confidence in Scripture extends to individual words and grammatical forms, not merely to the overall theological message. If Jesus, who is truth (John 14:6), treats individual words and grammatical details of Scripture as carrying argumentative weight, a doctrine of inerrancy limited to theological assertions does not match his practice.

> **P1:** In Matthew 22:31-32, Jesus builds a theological argument on the present tense of a single verb in Exodus 3:6.
>
> **P2:** In John 10:35, Jesus argues from a single word in Psalm 82:6 and adds the categorical statement "Scripture cannot be broken."
>
> **P3:** In Matthew 5:18, Jesus ties the permanence of Scripture to its individual letters and punctuation marks, not to its theological themes.
>
> **C:** Jesus' argumentative practice treats the details of Scripture, including individual words and grammatical forms, as carrying authoritative weight, which is consistent with plenary verbal inerrancy and inconsistent with limiting inerrancy to theological assertions.

## Argument 3: The Entailment from Divine Authorship

The argument runs from the character of God to the character of his word. God cannot lie (Titus 1:2; Numbers 23:19; Hebrews 6:18). Scripture is the product of divine inspiration, meaning God is its ultimate author (2 Timothy 3:16; 2 Peter 1:20-21). If God is the author and God cannot lie, Scripture cannot contain falsehood. This argument does not require that inspiration was dictation or that the human authors were not genuinely involved; it requires only that God's superintendence of the process was effective enough that the product reflects his character. A God who inspired a text that contains errors, even incidental ones, either did not effectively superintend the process or does not hold the truthfulness of incidental historical details to be part of his own character. Neither option is theologically acceptable.

> **P1:** Titus 1:2 and Numbers 23:19 state that God cannot lie; his character is constitutively truthful in all assertions.
>
> **P2:** 2 Timothy 3:16 and 2 Peter 1:20-21 attribute Scripture's origin to God's breath and Spirit, making God the ultimate author.
>
> **P3:** The character of the author determines the character of the product: a product of a perfectly truthful author will not contain false assertions, incidental or otherwise.
>
> **C:** The deductive entailment from God's truthfulness to Scripture's inerrancy requires no additional premise beyond the inspiration claim itself; limited inerrancy breaks the entailment by allowing that God effectively produced a text containing errors.

## Argument 4: The Rogers-McKim Thesis Is Historically Refuted

Limited inerrantists have argued (Rogers and McKim, 1979) that plenary inerrancy was a modern invention of the Princeton theology (Hodge and Warfield) and that the church historically affirmed only infallibility in matters of faith and practice. John Woodbridge's historical response (*Biblical Authority*, 1982) documented that Augustine, Aquinas, Luther, Calvin, and the mainstream of the patristic and Reformation traditions held a view of Scripture's truthfulness that extended to historical and factual matters, not merely to soteriological content. Augustine wrote: "I have learned to yield this respect and honour only to the canonical books of Scripture: of these alone do I most firmly believe that the authors were completely free from error." The Rogers-McKim proposal is not an accurate history; it is a retrojection of a modern distinction onto the pre-modern tradition.

> **P1:** Rogers and McKim argued that pre-modern Christian tradition affirmed only a functionally limited infallibility, not plenary inerrancy, making inerrancy a modern Princeton invention.
>
> **P2:** Woodbridge's historical research documented that Augustine, Aquinas, Luther, and Calvin all held that Scripture's truthfulness extended to factual and historical matters, not only to salvific content.
>
> **P3:** Augustine's explicit statement that the canonical authors were "completely free from error" is not compatible with a limited inerrancy position.
>
> **C:** The Rogers-McKim proposal fails as history; plenary inerrancy is the position of the mainstream Christian tradition, not a modern invention, and limited inerrancy is the departure from the tradition rather than its recovery.

## Position A -- Critique of Position B

### Critique 1: The Affirmation-Assumption Distinction Transfers Authority to the Interpreter

Limited inerrancy depends on distinguishing what Scripture intends to affirm from what it merely assumes, reports, or accommodates. The Bible intends to teach salvation; it does not intend to teach ancient Near Eastern chronology or ancient cosmology, so errors in those domains do not impugn its truthfulness. The problem is that this distinction is made by the interpreter, not by the text. The text does not signal which of its assertions are intentional affirmations and which are incidental assumptions. The interpreter applies external criteria to sort the assertions, which means the interpreter's judgment rather than the text's authority determines what Scripture teaches. This transfers the locus of authority from Scripture to the interpreter, which is precisely what a doctrine of Scripture's authority is supposed to prevent.

> **P1:** The distinction between intended affirmations and incidental assumptions is not marked in the text; it is applied by the interpreter using criteria external to the text.
>
> **P2:** If the interpreter determines which assertions fall within the scope of inerrancy and which do not, the interpreter rather than the text becomes the effective authority.
>
> **P3:** A doctrine of scriptural authority that subordinates the text's claims to the interpreter's categorization has undermined the authority it was meant to establish.
>
> **C:** Limited inerrancy's affirmation-assumption distinction transfers epistemic authority from Scripture to the interpreter and undermines the very doctrine of scriptural authority it claims to preserve.

### Critique 2: The Slope from Limited Inerrancy to Broader Erosion Is Historically Documented

Harold Lindsell documented in *The Battle for the Bible* (1976) that institutions and scholars who abandoned plenary inerrancy consistently moved toward positions that were more theologically liberal over the following decades. The mechanism is straightforward: once the interpreter is empowered to identify incidental errors in Scripture, the category of the incidental expands. What begins as accommodation to ancient cosmology extends to accommodation to ancient moral assumptions, which extends to accommodation to ancient views on gender, sexuality, and atonement. The limiting principle that was supposed to keep errors to the incidental has no principled stopping point, because the affirmation-assumption distinction applies wherever the interpreter judges the text to be out of step with contemporary knowledge or sensibility.

> **P1:** Identifying the category of incidental error grants the interpreter a tool with no principled limiting principle; the category can expand indefinitely.
>
> **P2:** Historical documentation shows that institutions departing from plenary inerrancy moved toward broader theological liberalism over subsequent decades.
>
> **P3:** The mechanism is the same in each case: the interpreter judges a claim to be incidental and therefore exempt from inerrancy, and the category of the incidental expands with changing cultural and intellectual pressures.
>
> **C:** Limited inerrancy has no principled stopping point; the historical record shows the category of incidental error expanding into theological content, which confirms the plenary inerrantist's concern.

### Critique 3: The Autographs Limitation Is Not Vacuous

Limited inerrantists argue that restricting inerrancy to the autographs makes it unfalsifiable and therefore empty. Plenary inerrantists respond that the autographs limitation reflects a genuine theological point: inspiration attaches to the act of divine authorship, not to the transmission process. The claim is not designed to be unfalsifiable; it is a precise claim about what the doctrine covers. Furthermore, the textual critical tradition has established with high confidence the text of the vast majority of the biblical documents, and the Chicago Statement recognizes that the copies we have are sufficiently reliable for all purposes to which God intended Scripture to be put. The autographs limitation is a precision, not a retreat.

> **P1:** Inspiration is an act of divine authorship; it attaches to the original act of writing, not to the subsequent process of copying and transmission.
>
> **P2:** Textual criticism has established the text of the biblical documents with sufficient confidence that the extant manuscripts function as reliable witnesses to the autographic text.
>
> **P3:** The autographs limitation is a theological precision about the scope of inspiration, not an apologetic escape hatch designed to protect inerrancy from empirical test.
>
> **C:** The autographs limitation is a principled theological claim about the scope of divine authorship; its practical inaccessibility does not make it vacuous, and the transmitted text is reliable enough to function as the Word of God for all practical purposes.

## Position A -- Rebuttals to Position B

### Rebuttal 1: The Phenomena Objection Revisited

Limited inerrantists argue that the phenomena of Scripture (discrepancies, divergent parallels, approximate quotations) force a limited inerrancy position. Plenary inerrantists respond that none of the standard phenomena objections are decisive. Numerical discrepancies between parallel accounts are typically textual transmission issues, rounding conventions, or different counting methods rather than authorial errors. Divergent wording of the same speech in the Synoptics reflects the ancient historiographical convention of reproducing the substance and gist of a speech rather than a verbatim transcript, a convention the biblical authors shared. New Testament quotations of the Old Testament that diverge from the Hebrew follow the Septuagint, which the New Testament authors regarded as a faithful Greek version. None of these phenomena require positing authorial error; they require attending to the literary conventions of the genres and cultures involved.

> **P1:** Numerical discrepancies in parallel accounts are typically attributable to textual transmission, rounding, or different counting methods rather than authorial error.
>
> **P2:** Divergent wording of speeches in the Synoptics reflects the ancient biographical and historiographical convention of reproducing the substance of a speech, not a verbatim transcript.
>
> **P3:** New Testament divergence from the Hebrew Old Testament in quotations reflects use of the Septuagint as an authoritative Greek version, not misquotation or careless citation.
>
> **C:** The phenomena of Scripture are explicable within a plenary inerrancy framework by attending to genre, literary convention, and textual transmission without positing authorial error.

### Rebuttal 2: The Purpose-of-Inspiration Argument Revisited

Limited inerrantists argue that 2 Timothy 3:15-17 ties inspiration to soteriological purpose, limiting the scope of inerrancy to what serves that purpose. Plenary inerrantists respond that the passage does not say inspiration is limited to salvific content; it says that the inspired Scripture is profitable for the purposes listed. The scope of inspiration (all Scripture is God-breathed) and the purposes of inspiration (profitable for teaching, reproof, correction, training) are distinct propositions in the passage. The purposes listed do not limit the scope of what is inspired; they describe what the inspired Scripture accomplishes. A complete physician's reference is authoritative in all of its content even though its purpose is to heal; the purpose does not limit the scope of its truthfulness.

> **P1:** 2 Timothy 3:16 states that "all Scripture is God-breathed and profitable"; the scope of inspiration and the purpose of inspiration are distinct propositions in the same verse.
>
> **P2:** The purposes listed (teaching, reproof, correction, training) describe what the inspired Scripture accomplishes; they do not restrict the scope of what is inspired to those subjects only.
>
> **P3:** A reference work can be authoritative in all of its content while being designed for a specific purpose; purpose does not limit the scope of truthfulness.
>
> **C:** The purpose-of-inspiration argument reads a limiting implication into 2 Timothy 3:15-17 that the text itself does not state; the scope of inspiration is explicitly "all Scripture," not "the soteriologically relevant portions of Scripture."

# Position B: Limited Inerrancy / Infallibility: Strongest Case

---

## Argument 1: The Phenomena of Scripture Require a Limited Account

The biblical texts present phenomena that resist harmonization without implausible strain. 2 Samuel 24:1 states that God incited David to number Israel; 1 Chronicles 21:1 attributes the same incitement to Satan. 2 Kings 8:26 gives Ahaziah's age as 22 when he became king; 2 Chronicles 22:2 gives it as 42. Matthew 27:9-10 attributes a quotation to Jeremiah that is actually from Zechariah 11:12-13. Acts 7:16 states that Jacob was buried at Shechem in the field Abraham bought from the sons of Hamor; Genesis places that purchase by Jacob, not Abraham. The harmonizations proposed for these passages require positing copyist errors, multiple manuscripts, different counting methods, and interpretive maneuvers that are all possible but cumulatively implausible. A doctrine of inspiration that generates a permanent apologetic project of harmonization is not a doctrine derived from the texts; it is a prior commitment forcing the texts into a shape they do not naturally take.

> **P1:** Specific textual phenomena including 2 Samuel 24:1 vs. 1 Chronicles 21:1, 2 Kings 8:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 22:2, and Matthew 27:9-10 present apparent factual discrepancies.
>
> **P2:** The harmonizations proposed for these texts require cumulative implausible maneuvers: copyist errors, variant manuscripts, alternative counting methods, and forced interpretations.
>
> **P3:** A doctrine of inerrancy that requires permanent harmonistic apologetics for phenomena that read naturally as errors is a doctrine under perpetual evidential pressure.
>
> **C:** The phenomena of Scripture fit more naturally with a limited inerrancy that treats incidental discrepancies as genuine minor errors than with a plenary inerrancy that must harmonize every apparent error at any cost.

## Argument 2: The Ancient Authorial Convention Argument

The biblical authors wrote within ancient literary and historiographical conventions that did not share modern precision norms. Ancient historians routinely rounded numbers, reported speeches in summarized paraphrase, used topical rather than chronological arrangement, adapted sources without citation, and wrote from a geocentric cosmological framework without intending to make scientific claims. Plenary inerrancy does not require that Scripture meet modern precision standards in these areas, which is why the Chicago Statement includes a careful article on genre and literary convention. But if plenary inerrancy accommodates all of these conventions, the functional difference between plenary inerrancy and limited inerrancy narrows to a terminological dispute about whether the convention-consistent departures from modern precision count as errors. Limited inerrancy simply names those departures honestly rather than multiplying interpretive categories to avoid the word.

> **P1:** Ancient authors routinely used rounded numbers, paraphrased speeches, topical rather than chronological arrangement, and geocentric cosmological language as standard literary conventions.
>
> **P2:** Plenary inerrantists accommodate all of these conventions within the doctrine, reducing the gap between plenary and limited inerrancy to the question of whether convention-consistent departures from precision count as errors.
>
> **P3:** Naming those departures honestly as minor errors where the conventions do not fully account for them is simpler and more transparent than multiplying interpretive categories to preserve the label of inerrancy.
>
> **C:** The gap between plenary and limited inerrancy is narrower than it appears once ancient literary conventions are fully accounted for; limited inerrancy names the residual departures honestly rather than through interpretive strain.

## Argument 3: The Humanity of Scripture Requires Genuine Human Authorship

The biblical authors were not passive conduits; they were genuine human authors with individual styles, limited knowledge, and cultural situatedness. Luke explicitly says he investigated his subject carefully and wrote an orderly account (Luke 1:1-4). Paul distinguishes his own opinion from the Lord's command (1 Corinthians 7:12). The author of Ecclesiastes reasons from within a finite, death-shadowed perspective. If verbal plenary inspiration extends to the selection of every word, the human authors' researches, opinions, and limited perspectives are at best superficial features of the text and at worst misleading. A more adequate account of inspiration holds that God worked through the full humanity of the authors, which means working through their knowledge and its limits, producing a text that is genuinely human and genuinely divine, infallible in its saving purpose without requiring modern precision standards in every incidental detail.

> **P1:** Luke 1:1-4 presents Luke as a careful investigator who compiled his account from sources and interviews, a genuinely human authorial process.
>
> **P2:** 1 Corinthians 7:12 presents Paul distinguishing his own judgment from a divine command, indicating that his human judgment has a place in Scripture's composition.
>
> **P3:** If inspiration extends to every word such that the human author's research, opinion, and limited perspective are wholly overridden, the Bible's genuine humanity is reduced to a surface feature, which contradicts the evidence of the texts themselves.
>
> **C:** Genuine human authorship requires that the human authors' knowledge and its limits be real features of the text; inspiration works through that humanity rather than overriding it, which is more compatible with limited inerrancy than with strict verbal plenary inspiration.

## Argument 4: Limited Inerrancy Preserves the Doctrine's Apologetic Function

A doctrine of plenary inerrancy is permanently exposed to refutation by any apparent error that resists harmonization. The history of evangelical apologetics shows a pattern of successive harmonizations followed by the discovery of new apparent errors, which generates a permanent apologetic treadmill. Limited inerrancy insulates the doctrine's core claims from this exposure: the Bible's truthfulness in what it intends to teach regarding salvation and Christian living is not threatened by ancient chronological conventions or textual discrepancies in minor historical details. The result is a more durable doctrine: confident on the matters that actually bear on Christian faith and life, not permanently exposed to refutation by the discovery of ancient historical inaccuracies.

> **P1:** Plenary inerrancy is exposed to refutation by any historical or factual discrepancy that resists harmonization; the apologetic treadmill is permanent.
>
> **P2:** Limited inerrancy insulates the doctrine's theologically significant claims from exposure to historical or scientific counterevidence.
>
> **P3:** A doctrine that is durable under evidential pressure and honest about the phenomena is more apologetically useful than one that requires defending every incidental detail.
>
> **C:** Limited inerrancy is apologetically stronger for the claims that matter most: Scripture's truthfulness in matters of salvation, ethics, and Christian life is preserved without entailing a defense of every ancient historical and numerical detail.

## Position B -- Critique of Position A

### Critique 1: Plenary Inerrancy Conflates Infallibility and Inerrancy

The category of infallibility, that Scripture unfailingly accomplishes its purpose, is distinct from inerrancy, that Scripture contains no false assertion. Infallibility is a claim about Scripture's efficacy; inerrancy is a claim about its propositional accuracy. The pre-modern tradition, including Augustine and Calvin, affirmed infallibility and used language about Scripture's truthfulness in a way that was oriented toward its saving function. Warfield's innovation was to define inerrancy in terms of propositional accuracy in all subject matters, which is a more precise and demanding claim than the tradition required. Limited inerrancy recovers the traditional category of infallibility while being precise about what the biblical data and the theological argument actually require.

> **P1:** Infallibility (Scripture unfailingly achieves its saving purpose) and inerrancy (Scripture contains no false proposition) are distinct claims.
>
> **P2:** The pre-modern tradition's language about Scripture's truthfulness was primarily oriented toward its saving efficacy rather than propositional accuracy in all subject matters.
>
> **P3:** Warfield's formulation of inerrancy as propositional accuracy in all assertions is a more demanding claim than the tradition required and than the theological argument from inspiration entails.
>
> **C:** Plenary inerrancy as the Chicago Statement defines it is a stronger claim than infallibility and stronger than the tradition or the theological argument requires; limited inerrancy is the more precisely calibrated position.

### Critique 2: The Deductive Argument from Divine Authorship Is Too Strong

Plenary inerrantists argue: God cannot lie; God authored Scripture; therefore Scripture cannot contain error. Limited inerrantists respond that the argument proves too much. If the logic holds, it should apply to every word spoken by God's servants under his inspiration, including the speeches of Job's friends (which God explicitly says are not right, Job 42:7), the reported speech of Satan (Genesis 3:4-5), and the Psalmist's imprecations. These are all in Scripture, and Scripture contains them accurately, but they are not all true assertions by the inspired author. Scripture accurately reports false assertions, mistaken views, and diabolical speech. The deductive argument from divine authorship must be refined to apply to the author's own assertions, not to everything contained in the text, which is precisely the distinction limited inerrancy uses.

> **P1:** Scripture contains reported speech that is explicitly false: Satan's lie in Genesis 3:4-5, the wrong counsel of Job's friends (Job 42:7), and diabolical temptation.
>
> **P2:** The deductive argument from divine authorship cannot apply to these contents without making God the author of false assertions in Scripture.
>
> **P3:** The deductive argument must be restricted to the author's own intended assertions, not to everything contained in the text, which is the same distinction limited inerrancy uses when distinguishing intended affirmations from incidental assumptions.
>
> **C:** Plenary inerrancy already implicitly uses the intended-assertion distinction to handle reported speech; limiting inerrancy to intended affirmations is a consistent application of the same principle, not a novel departure.

## Position B -- Rebuttals to Position A

### Rebuttal 1: The Slope Argument Is Not an Argument

The plenary inerrantist claim that limited inerrancy leads to broader liberalism is a sociological observation, not a logical argument. The slope from limited inerrancy to theological liberalism is not logically entailed; it is contingently correlated in certain institutional histories. Many limited inerrantists including I. Howard Marshall, F.F. Bruce, and R.P. Martin maintained orthodox Christology, the bodily resurrection, and substitutionary atonement alongside their position on Scripture. The limiting principle does not expand automatically; it expands when interpreters who have already departed from orthodox theology use it as a tool. The tool is not the cause; the prior theological departure is the cause.

> **P1:** The logical entailment from limited inerrancy to theological liberalism does not hold; many limited inerrantists maintained full orthodox theology.
>
> **P2:** Marshall, Bruce, and Martin are examples of scholars holding limited inerrancy alongside full Christological and soteriological orthodoxy.
>
> **P3:** The institutional slide from limited inerrancy to liberalism occurs when interpreters who have already departed from orthodoxy use the limited inerrancy framework; the departure precedes and causes the slide, not the other way around.
>
> **C:** The slippery slope argument is a sociological generalization, not a logical entailment; limited inerrancy does not logically require theological liberalism, and the historical examples of orthodox limited inerrantists demonstrate this.

### Rebuttal 2: Jesus' Use of Scripture Revisited

Plenary inerrantists argue that Jesus' precise use of Old Testament details (present tense verbs, individual words) implies verbal plenary inerrancy. Limited inerrantists respond that Jesus' use of Scripture demonstrates its authority and reliability for the purposes he employed it; it does not imply that every incidental historical detail is errorless. Jesus' argument in Matthew 22 turns on what Exodus teaches about the nature of God's relationship to the patriarchs; he uses the grammatical detail to establish a theological point. That demonstrates that Scripture's theological assertions are reliable enough to bear argumentative weight; it does not require that every numerical figure in every historical narrative is errorless. The scope of his confidence matches the scope of his argument.

> **P1:** Jesus' precise use of Old Testament grammatical details demonstrates the reliability of Scripture's theological assertions for the purposes he employed them.
>
> **P2:** The scope of his confidence is the scope of the argument: the theological point about resurrection is established by the text's theological witness, not by incidental historical details.
>
> **P3:** Using a text's grammatical detail to establish a theological point does not imply that every incidental historical detail in every text is errorless; it implies that the text is reliable for its theological purpose.
>
> **C:** Jesus' use of Scripture demonstrates its reliability for theological argumentation; it does not require plenary inerrancy extending to incidental historical and numerical details.

# The Three Actual Hinges

The first hinge is **the affirmation-assumption distinction and who controls it**. Plenary inerrancy argues that no principled line exists between intended affirmations and incidental assumptions without giving the interpreter control over the scope of Scripture's authority. Limited inerrancy argues that the distinction is the same one plenary inerrancy already uses to handle reported false speech and that genre, context, and authorial purpose provide principled criteria for drawing it. Whether the criteria are principled enough to prevent interpretive creep is the real question, and neither side has fully resolved it.

The second hinge is **the phenomena of Scripture and whether they constitute genuine errors**. Both sides agree that ancient literary conventions, rounding, summarized speech, and topical arrangement account for many apparent discrepancies. The question is whether the residual cases, those that remain after the conventions are fully applied, are genuine errors or undiscovered harmonizations. Plenary inerrantists maintain confidence that harmonizations exist and are to be expected given the limitations of current knowledge. Limited inerrantists hold that cumulative implausible harmonization is itself evidence that the inerrancy commitment is driving the exegesis rather than the other way around. Neither side can prove its case on the residual cases without a completed harmonization project that does not exist.

The third hinge is **the autographs limitation and whether it is theologically meaningful or apologetically vacuous**. Plenary inerrantists argue it is a precise claim about the scope of divine authorship. Limited inerrantists argue that a doctrine of Scripture's truthfulness that can never be tested against any accessible document is performing no epistemic function. This dispute is partly about the purpose of a doctrine of inerrancy: if it is a deductive entailment from God's character to his word, the autographs limitation is appropriate; if it is a claim about the document we actually use and rely on, it is vacuous. The two sides have different accounts of what the doctrine is for, and that prior disagreement drives the autographs dispute.
